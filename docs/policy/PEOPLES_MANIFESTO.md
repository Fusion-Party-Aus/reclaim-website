# Victoria Belongs To Victorians

**They sold your train station. They sold your toll road. They sold your lottery. Now they want your vote — by promising to argue about the bill a little differently. We're not here to argue about the bill. We're here to take back what's ours.**

---

## This Is Not A Budget Document. This Is A Promise.

Every train line in this state was built with your money. Every station, every tram corridor, every tunnel under the city — paid for by Victorians, generation after generation, on the promise that it belonged to all of us.

It doesn't anymore.

A private consortium owns the shops at Southern Cross Station. A toll company owns the road into the city until 2045. A gambling corporation owns the right to run Victoria's lottery until 2068. Every single day, money that should be yours walks straight past you and into a shareholder report on the other side of the world.

Take that lottery deal on its own. In May 2026, the government quietly handed Tattersall's Sweeps a 40-year extension out to 2068 — and the upfront premium payment, $1.145 billion, landed just in time to cover almost the entire 2026-27 surplus the government's been campaigning on. The Treasurer says the surplus doesn't depend on it. The Auditor-General is reviewing whether the deal got Victoria a fair price. We don't know yet whether the timing was coincidence or convenience. What we do know, based on how profitable this company's lottery business already is, is that Victoria likely handed away a revenue stream worth one and a half to two and a half times what it got paid for it — a 40-year gambling monopoly sold for a one-year accounting win, and that's a bad trade either way.

This is the plan to take it back. All of it. Not eventually — starting now.

---

## The Lie We've All Been Told

For thirty years, two parties have told you the same lie in two different accents.

One says: borrow more, spend more, and don't ask where the money from what we already built went. The other says: borrow less, cut more, and don't ask where the money from what we already built went. Neither one will say the sentence that actually matters, because saying it means admitting what they did:

**We sold the income from your infrastructure to private companies, and we're still paying the bill.**

That's not an accident. That's not bad luck. That's not "complex economic circumstances." That is two political parties, for three decades, finding it easier to hand a contract to a corporate mate than to do the harder, slower work of running something publicly and well. They built the trains. They handed the revenue away. You got the debt. Someone else got the dividend.

We're done asking nicely for that to stop.

## What We're Actually Going To Do

### Every station becomes yours again

When a private operator's contract runs out, it does not get renewed. Not Southern Cross. Not Metro Trains. Not Yarra Trams. Not the bus companies. The state takes it back, runs it, and keeps every dollar of revenue that used to leave the building in a consultant's briefcase.

No payouts. No "fair compensation" for companies that have been extracting public wealth for decades. When the contract ends, it ends. If a company has been failing to meet its obligations — and the record on this is not subtle — we don't wait for the contract to expire. We terminate for cause, today, and we take the asset back for nothing, because a company that already broke its word is not owed a cent for the privilege of losing the deal.

### We build a public developer that builds homes nobody else will, and we never sell what we build

A public fund — owned by Victorians, run for Victorians, answerable to no developer's shareholders — builds housing on every piece of land near a train station the state already owns. Tens of thousands of homes. Then tens of thousands more. Then more again, because every home we finish pays for the next ten.

We don't build it to sell it to the highest bidder. We build it to keep it, forever, and rent it at a price that doesn't bankrupt the person paying it. The state becomes a landlord that doesn't want to gouge you, because the whole point of the landlord is you.

### Land near a train station stops being a casino chip

If you own a car park or an empty lot next to a station the public just spent billions upgrading, and you're sitting on it waiting for the value to climb while doing nothing with it — that ends. Every year you sit on it, it costs you more. Build something, sell to someone who will, or sell it to the state. Land-banking near public infrastructure has been a guaranteed win for speculators for decades. We're done subsidising people who do nothing.

This does not touch your home. It does not touch a family's house, a duplex, anyone's place to live. This is aimed at people sitting on dead land for profit while a housing crisis happens around them. Nobody loses their home. Speculators lose the free ride.

### We out-build the developers who've been ripping you off

The state doesn't ban private developers. It doesn't need to. It builds the same thing they build, at cost, with no profit margin skimmed off the top, and sells or rents it for less. If your business model depends on charging people as much as the market will bear for a home, you now have to compete with a landlord that doesn't need a profit. Beat that price, or lose the customer. That's not socialism. That's exactly what Medicare does to private health insurance and what Australia Post does to private couriers, and nobody calls those radical.

### Every Victorian gets a cheque

This is the part that matters most, so we'll say it plainly: once the state owns these assets and they're earning money, a share of that money comes back to you. Not as a tax credit. Not as a rebate you have to apply for. A direct cash payment, every year, the way Alaska has paid every one of its residents a share of its oil wealth since 1982.

Not someday. Starting with the first tenants in the first homes the public fund builds, and growing every year after that, until it reaches every Victorian.

At full scale, once this fund is doing what it's built to do, that dividend — stacked with what a household saves from below-market rent and not needing a car because they live somewhere actually walkable — is worth somewhere between four hundred and well over a thousand dollars a week. If the federal government ever delivers a basic income on top of that, a Victorian household isn't choosing between rent and groceries anymore. They're the first people in the country who get to stop counting.

That is not a rounding error on a press release. That is what happens when a state stops giving its wealth away.

### Stop pretending crime is the problem instead of the symptom

Every politician in this state wants to talk about bail laws. None of them want to talk about why a generation of kids grew up in a rental market that evicts them every twelve months, in a housing market that locked them out before they turned eighteen, in a state that built infrastructure and gave the profits to someone else instead of using them to make life affordable. Housing insecurity and cost-of-living desperation are the actual upstream cause of the crime wave everyone's so loud about. Fix the economics and you fix more of the crime problem than another decade of "tougher on bail" ever will. We're the only ones willing to say that out loud, because we're the only ones not protecting the system that caused it.

## Why Nobody Else Will Do This

Because doing this means admitting what they did. Labor signed the contracts. The Coalition wants to sign more of them, just with different beneficiaries. Both of them have spent thirty years treating a handful of corporate partners — toll operators, franchise holders, gambling companies, big developers — as more important to keep happy than the people who actually live here and actually vote.

That's not a conspiracy theory. You don't need a conspiracy. You just need three decades of a system where it was always easier, always safer, always more career-friendly for the people in charge to renew the contract than to build the public alternative. Every time. Until renewing the contract became the only thing either party knows how to do.

We're not interested in managing that system more politely. We're interested in ending it.

---

## What We're Not Promising

We're not going to pretend this fixes everything overnight, because the people who do that are lying to you, and we'd rather you trust us in five years than believe us for five minutes.

The pension fix doesn't put a single new dollar in this year's budget — it stops the state from having to borrow new money for an old promise the wrong way, and that's still real. The public housing fund doesn't deliver a hundred thousand homes on day one — it delivers tens of thousands in the first wave, and compounds from there, because every home that's finished pays for the next one. The dividend doesn't arrive in everyone's bank account next financial year — it starts with the people living in the first homes we build, and it grows from there until it reaches everyone.

What we are promising is the direction. Every dollar this plan borrows buys something the public keeps. Every contract we don't renew is income that comes home. Every home we build, we keep. That's the whole idea, and it doesn't change depending on which year you're asking about.

## One Seat Can Start This

You don't need to win government to force this onto the table. One seat with a clear mandate and the willingness to use procedural leverage — refusing supply, refusing to wave through the next thirty-year contract, demanding the public answers nobody else is demanding — can make every contract renewal a fight instead of a formality.

That's not a small thing. That's how thirty years of giving it away started losing.

## The Whole Argument, In One Line

**Every dollar Victoria borrows from now on should buy something Victorians keep — and everything we already own should start paying us back, starting now, not someday.**

That's it. That's the whole pitch. Everything else is just the plan for how we do it.
