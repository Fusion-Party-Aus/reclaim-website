# Fusion Victoria Policy Omnibus

_Version 3.0 — last revised 17 June 2026_

**Victoria didn't borrow too much. It gave away the income from what it built — and this plan takes it back, then pays you a dividend.**

## TL;DR (For The Group Chat)

For thirty years, Victoria borrowed money to build infrastructure, then handed the profits to private operators while taxpayers carried the debt. This paper closes that loop: clear out the worst debt, build assets the state keeps, and pay the dividend straight to Victorians.

It answers both sides of the budget debate. Labor's 2026-27 Budget says net debt is falling as a share of the economy — true, but the dollar figure itself still climbs from $175.6 billion to $199.3 billion over four years, and its cost-of-living relief expires on an election timetable. The Coalition's "Ten Year Plan" calls that figure a "corruption tax" and proposes a hiring freeze, abolished taxes, and a surplus that doesn't land until 2032 — while cutting land tax, the exact lever this paper says needs to point the other way. Both sides argue about the size of the number. This one is about who gets the income from what the number already bought.

The short version:

- **Refinance the pension cliff** the way NYC's Mamdani administration just did: stretch a panicked 2035 repayment deadline to a sane 2041 one, cutting the new borrowing the state would otherwise need purely to fund an escalating super top-up by $1.2–1.5 billion a year at its peak — not new operating cash, but real headroom in the borrowing program — without touching anyone's super.

- **Stop building housing the state immediately loses money on.** Instead, set up a Singapore-style wealth fund (the VWHF) that builds mixed-income housing and commercial precincts around train stations, and keeps the rent forever.

- **Make land-banking near stations a losing bet.** A cumulative surcharge on commercial surface car parks and other single-use commercial sites within 1 km of a station — explicitly excluding people's homes — rises every single year a site stays single-use, not a flat fee, a compounding one. Build mixed-use, sell to someone who will, or sell to the state. Statewide, every commercial surface car park either gets consolidated into a structured facility or pays an ongoing surcharge — hide the parking or pay for the land it's wasting. And because rezoning one of these sites into a development site instantly re-rates its value on the state's books, the debt used to build on it is covered before construction even starts.

- **The state out-competes private developers instead of fighting them.** The VWHF builds housing and retail at cost — no profit margin, no shareholder return, no marketing budget — and prices it just under market. Like Australia Post does to couriers, or Medicare does to private health insurers: the state sets the floor, and the private sector either matches it or loses customers to it.

- **Strip $1 billion a year of waste** out of the privatised rail, tram, bus and tollway model.

- **Take back Southern Cross Station for $0** when its contract lapses, hand operations to the VWHF, and keep the commercial revenue Civic Nexus currently pockets — covering the station's running costs with no net increase in general government spending.

- **Close the loop**: once the state owns these retail precincts, lease them the way Westfield does — rent plus a cut of turnover — and use the surplus to fund an annual cash dividend to every Victorian.

- **Get ahead of the next Southern Cross before it happens.** The Metro Tunnel's five new underground stations just opened under a 25-year availability-payment PPP with the Cross Yarra Partnership — lock their retail precincts into the VWHF model now, before they become the next thirty-year leasing arrangement a future Fusion MP has to spend a PAEC hearing clawing back.

- **Why this is also a crime policy**: cost of living and housing insecurity are the upstream cause of Victoria's number one polling issue. Fix the economics and you take the air out of the crime debate without "tougher on bail" theatrics.

Everything below is the long version, with sourcing.

## How Victoria Got Here: The Kennett Playbook

**To understand why this plan works the way it does, you need to know how Victoria got here.**

To understand why this document proposes what it does, you need the backstory.

In the early 1990s, the collapse of the State Bank of Victoria — via its merchant banking arm, Tricontinental — left the state carrying billions in bad debt, and Victoria's credit rating was downgraded. The Kennett government's answer was the largest privatisation program in Australian history: the electricity generators and distributors, the gas network, the Met trains and trams (split into competing private franchises), CityLink, prisons and more were sold off or handed to private operators on long-term contracts, with the proceeds used to pay down state debt.

It worked, in the narrow sense that the debt numbers improved through the 1990s. But it set the template for everything that followed: when Victoria needs infrastructure, the state borrows to build it — or lets a private consortium borrow to build it — and a private operator captures the recurring revenue, fares, retail rent, advertising, tollway charges, for the life of a multi-decade contract. The state carries the construction debt and the political risk; the operator carries the cash flow.

Thirty years on, net debt is projected to climb from $175.6 billion in 2026-27 to $199.3 billion by 2029-30, according to the 2026-27 Budget's own forward estimates. Privatisation didn't fix the underlying problem, it just changed who collects the rent on the way through. Metro Trains, Yarra Trams, Civic Nexus at Southern Cross, and the toll road operators on CityLink and the West Gate Tunnel are all downstream of that same 1990s decision.

This document isn't an argument for privatisation 2.0, and it isn't reflexive nationalisation either. It's an argument that the next thirty years of asset decisions should run the other way: when the state builds something, the state should own the upside.

## Three Arguments About the Same $199 Billion

**Labor and the Coalition are both arguing about the size of Victoria's debt. Neither is asking who gets the income from what that debt already bought.**

Two documents define the mainstream debate about that figure, and it's worth being precise about what each actually says, because Fusion's pitch only makes sense once you see what both of them leave out.

### The Allan Labor Government's 2026-27 Budget

leads with cost-of-living relief, free and half-price public transport, vehicle registration rebates, energy bill rebates, funded against an operating position the government describes as back on track: a $0.7 billion operating surplus in 2025-26, growing toward $2 billion by 2029-30, and net debt as a share of the state economy (GSP) projected to fall every year from 2026-27 onward, from 24.9% to 24.4%. On the government's own numbers, that's "Step 3" of its five-step fiscal strategy delivered, and "Step 4" and "Step 5" underway.

That framing is true, and also incomplete in exactly the way that lets the Coalition's attack land. Net debt to GSP falling doesn't mean net debt is falling, it rises every single year of the forward estimates, by almost $24 billion between 2026-27 and 2029-30. "Falling as a share of the economy" means the economy is growing faster than the debt, which is a real and legitimate fiscal achievement, but it's a number that can be true while the dollar figure people actually feel, through interest costs competing with the services budget, keeps climbing regardless. And the headline cost-of-living measures are, by design, temporary: free transport in April-May and half-price fares only until January 2027 are targeted relief timed to an election cycle, not structural fixes to the cost pressures themselves.

There's a sharper problem with the 2026-27 surplus specifically. In May 2026, the government granted Tattersall's Sweeps (a Lottery Corporation subsidiary) a 42-year extension of Victoria's Public Lottery Licence, out to 2068, in exchange for an upfront premium payment of roughly $1.145 billion, landing in two instalments during 2026-27. That's nearly the entire forecast 2026-27 operating surplus of about $1.05 billion, from a single one-off licence payment rather than from spending restraint or recurring revenue growth. The Treasurer has denied the surplus depends on it, and DTF hasn't published a figure attributing the surplus to the payment, so this isn't a settled number, but the Auditor-General is reviewing whether the deal "optimised value" for the state, and the opposition has made exactly this connection. Strip the one-off payment out and the 2026-27 "surplus" looks a lot closer to break-even, which matters for this paper's argument: Labor's "Step 3 delivered" claim is leaning on a 42-year forward sale of lottery revenue to make one year's number look better, which is the same kind of one-off-dressed-as-structural manoeuvre this document calls out the Coalition for elsewhere, just smaller and quieter.

It's also worth naming the road not taken, because it's the cleanest illustration of this paper's whole argument in miniature. Nothing compelled the government to grant Tattersall's Sweeps a 42-year extension at all; the licence could have lapsed, been re-tendered, or been brought in-house as a state-operated lottery. Gambling is a famously high-margin, low-volatility business, which is precisely why a private operator was willing to pay $1.145 billion up front for the right to run it for 42 years; the recurring profit a state-run lottery would throw off is worth considerably more than that lump sum, discounted out to 2068, and could have funded debt service or transport infrastructure as an ongoing annuity rather than a one-year sugar hit. That's "own the upside" applied to an asset the state already fully controlled, no acquisition required, just a decision not to sell. For Fusion's purposes the point is now academic: the licence is locked in for the next 42 years, so whatever sovereign lottery revenue Victoria gave away in May 2026 is off the table for the entire planning horizon of this document.

CityLink is the road version of the same story, and a much bigger one. Transurban's CityLink concession, already extended once to 2035, was extended again to 2045 as part of the West Gate Tunnel deal, handing a private toll operator another decade of one of the most lucrative road assets in the world in exchange for building infrastructure the state could have funded and tolled itself. CityLink tolls have cleared roughly $2 million a day since at least 2018 and have kept climbing with CPI-linked toll increases since, putting current daily revenue in the same ballpark as the lottery premium's entire annualised value, every single day, for another two decades. Unlike the lottery, this isn't hypothetical or in the past: CityLink doesn't revert to the state until 2045, so there's still a multi-decade asset sitting there generating private recurring revenue off a road corridor the public built and owns.

Fusion's own social media is already making this exact point in real time. A June 2026 post from the party's account calls out Transurban/Linkt for marketing a 26c/litre fuel discount as cost-of-living relief when unlocking it requires racking up ten separate toll trips, i.e. paying the monopoly operator more in order to receive its "discount." It's a small, consumer-facing example of the same structure as the lottery licence and CityLink's 2045 concession at state-budget scale: a private operator holding a government-granted monopoly gets to frame its own revenue mechanics as relief from cost-of-living pressures that the monopoly itself helps create.

And the comparison undersells what a state-owned CityLink could actually do. Transurban's toll take is priced to cover maintenance and renewal _and_ deliver a commercial return to shareholders on top, which is the entire point of a concession from the operator's perspective. A state owner doesn't need that margin: it could cut CityLink tolls by half, still fully fund ongoing maintenance and renewal out of the remaining revenue, and have money left over. Halving tolls on a road that hundreds of thousands of vehicles use every day would put more money back in commuters' pockets, every single day, than Linkt's 26c/litre discount could ever deliver, before even accounting for the fact that the discount requires paying tolls to earn it in the first place.

### The Liberal/Nationals "Ten Year Plan"

takes the same figure (rounded to "nearly $200 billion") and the roughly $9 billion a year, "$1 million an hour," interest bill, and proposes to fix it by 2032 through ending specific spending items, a hiring freeze on "back office roles" claimed to save $22 billion, eliminating what it calls $15 billion in "corruption payments" on Big Build projects, and "using windfall revenue to pay down debt, not increase spending." On the revenue side it simultaneously promises payroll tax relief, land tax relief, a stamp duty exemption for first home buyers up to $1 million, and abolishing the "Emergency Services Tax," the "Schools Tax," and the "GP Tax" — cutting several of the state's existing revenue bases at the same time it promises a cash surplus, on a six-year horizon, twice as far out as Labor's nearest surplus milestone.

Read those two lists side by side and the arithmetic doesn't close. The spending cuts are either rounding errors against $9 billion a year in interest (capping public service salaries saves $20 million _over a decade_) or contested figures with no department-by-department breakdown ($22 billion from a back-office hiring freeze; the $15 billion "corruption" figure is the same number used elsewhere to describe the entire decade's Big Build cost overruns, not a discrete pool of money sitting in an account waiting to be clawed back). Meanwhile the revenue side removes money from the same budget the plan claims to be repairing. "Use windfall revenue to pay down debt, not increase spending" is the closest the Coalition's plan comes to engaging with land value capture at all, and it points the opposite direction to the Windfall Gains Tax this paper builds on: where Victoria's actual WGT taxes rezoning uplift at up to 50%, the Coalition's "land tax relief" plank narrows the exact base this paper's Transit Capital Fund depends on.

### Where Fusion's pitch actually sits

Both major parties are arguing about the size of a number, and over what timeframe spending less or taxing less makes it smaller or grow slower. Neither is asking who captures the income generated by the assets that debt already paid for. Labor's surplus is real, but it coexists with a state that still hands Civic Nexus the commercial take at Southern Cross, still pays Metro Trains and Yarra Trams a margin on top of operating costs, and still lets land near new stations sit idle while its value compounds, untaxed, beyond the WGT's one-off trigger. The Coalition's plan would make all three of those worse: cutting land tax and redirecting infrastructure toward "local, community focused" projects instead of transit-oriented precincts means less, not more, of the value uplift from public infrastructure ever gets captured publicly in the first place.

Fusion's answer to "$199 billion and rising" isn't "spend less" (Labor, eventually) or "spend less and tax less" (the Coalition, on a longer timeline) — it's "change who the income from the next $199 billion of assets belongs to." The pension smoothing and transport efficiency measures below are a genuine, if modest, contribution to the debt figure both other parties are fixated on, roughly $2.1-2.4 billion a year once phased in, as the "How This Actually Pays Down Debt" section sets out. But the larger claim in this document isn't about that number at all. It's that thirty years of debating debt totals while private operators and landowners keep the income from publicly-funded infrastructure is the actual disease, and neither major party's plan touches it.

## Clearing $4 Billion: The Pension Cliff

**Victoria is legally locked into a panicked pension repayment schedule that forces it to borrow billions purely to hit a 2035 deadline. Smoothing that schedule to 2041 cuts that forced borrowing by up to $1.5 billion a year — without touching anyone's super.**

The single biggest line item in Mamdani's NYC budget balancing act was a $1.64 billion accounting adjustment: restructuring the city's unfunded pension liabilities to a "level dollar" amortization schedule over a longer period.

Victoria can mimic this by targeting the stateʼs massive defined-benefit superannuation liability, which heavily weighs down the balance sheet.

This mechanism directly replicates the core debt-clearing maneuver used by New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani in his Fiscal Year 2027 Executive Budget (released May 2026. Facing a massive deficit, the Mamdani administration worked with the state to pass enabling legislation to restructure the cityʼs unfunded pension liabilities. By transitioning the payment schedule to a fixed "level dollar" amortization model and extending the timeline out to 2037, the maneuver generated $1.64 billion USD in direct cash-flow relief for Fiscal Year 2027 alone, without cutting any retiree benefits — though, as the honest framing below makes clear, that near-term relief comes at the cost of several billion dollars in additional long-run nominal contributions.

According to Victorian Parliamentary Budget Office costings and recent Auditor-General reports, the Victorian Government is locked into an aggressive, front-loaded legislative mandate to eliminate its roughly $17-18 billion unfunded superannuation liability by 2035.

Because the government previously deferred a critical $3 billion payment, the state is now facing escalating "top-up" cash demands out of the consolidated fund to catch up and hit that 2035 cliff — the PBO costed the deferral alone as adding roughly $882.8 million to net debt, and the broader catch-up schedule layered on top pushes annual top-up demands into the hundreds of millions, climbing toward $1-2 billion a year as 2035 approaches. Sucking that scale of raw cash out of the operating budget during a period of record debt and high interest rates forces the state into a spiral of borrowing more capital just to fund daily cash obligations.

We should propose to legislate an amendment to the _State Superannuation Act_ to dismantle the volatile, front-loaded 2035 target. Shift the repayment framework to a smoothed, actuarially sound 15-year fixed "level dollar" amortization schedule extending to 2041.

**Be precise about what "frees up $1.2-1.5 billion a year" actually means, because an earlier version of this section got this wrong in a way that matters, and a rigorous fact-check correctly called it out.** Under the accounting standard that governs Australian public-sector budgets, AASB 1049 and the ABS Government Finance Statistics framework, contributions the government makes into the State Superannuation Fund's plan assets are a _balance-sheet transaction_, cash converting into superannuation plan assets, not an _operating expense_. They have essentially zero direct impact on the Net Operating Balance, the figure reported as the headline budget surplus or deficit. That means smoothing the amortisation schedule does **not** improve the operating surplus by $1.2-1.5 billion a year, and it does **not** free up "operating revenue" that could be redirected to hospitals, schools, transport or anything else funded out of the operating budget. Any version of this paper, or any Fusion MP, that frames this mechanism as putting $1.2-1.5 billion a year into the operating budget is making a claim the GFS accounting framework directly contradicts, and a hostile fact-check will win that exchange every time.

What the smoothing _does_ affect is narrower, but still real: the state's underlying cash balance and its general government borrowing requirement. Under the current 2035 target, the Auditor-General's Annual Financial Report confirms the state must contribute over $2 billion a year from 1 July 2027 to 30 June 2035 to hit that target, a cash outflow that, if not met from existing reserves, has to be met by issuing more debt. Smoothing the schedule to 2041 reduces that near-term cash contribution requirement, which reduces the near-term _debt-issuance_ the state would otherwise need to undertake to fund it. The $1.2-1.5 billion a year figure should be read as a reduction in the pace of new borrowing for this specific purpose, not as cash available for general spending. The underlying $17-18 billion unfunded liability remains exactly as unfunded either way; what changes is how fast the state is required to top it up, and therefore how much it has to borrow, in any given year between now and the target date.

Let's be upfront about the cost of this too, because the strongest attack on this mechanism is also the most accurate one, and the actuarial maths behind it checks out. The Reason Foundation described Mamdani's version of this manoeuvre as a "pension gimmick", and on the core mechanics they're right: stretching the amortisation schedule from 2035 to 2041 doesn't make the roughly $17-18 billion underlying liability smaller, it defers it, and the fund loses six extra years of compounding on the capital that would otherwise have been injected earlier. Modelling the lost compounding on a deferred contribution of roughly $1.35 billion a year (the midpoint of the $1.2-1.5 billion range), at a long-term fund earning rate around 6.5% net of the state's own borrowing cost around 4.5%, over the six-year extension from 2035 to 2041, produces a net loss to the balance sheet from foregone compounding of just over **$1.95 billion in nominal terms**, consistent with the order of magnitude the PBO found when it costed the earlier $3 billion deferral (roughly $882.8 million in added net debt for a smaller, earlier deferral over a shorter horizon). And because the state has already been required to find over $2 billion a year from 2027 under the 2035 schedule specifically _because_ of that earlier deferral, stretching the timeline further to 2041 means the catch-up cash requirements in the schedule's outer years escalate again, compounding on top of an already-compounded shortfall. This is not a one-off cost that nets out quickly; it's a roughly $2 billion addition to a liability that was already growing because of the last deferral.

"This costs more in the long run" is not the same as "this is a bad idea," but it does mean the honest case for this mechanism is much narrower than "frees up $1.2-1.5 billion a year for housing and debt retirement." The honest case is: (a) the underlying $17-18 billion liability is unfunded and growing regardless of which schedule is chosen, that's a pre-existing problem this paper didn't create and can't fix with an amortisation tweak; (b) the 2035 target, as currently legislated, requires the state to find over $2 billion a year in cash from 2027, funded by fresh general-government borrowing if the operating budget can't absorb it, which itself adds to net debt and interest costs; (c) smoothing to 2041 reduces that near-term borrowing requirement by roughly $1.2-1.5 billion a year at its peak, at the cost of an additional ~$1.95 billion in long-run nominal liability growth from lost compounding. The trade is real on both sides: less near-term debt issuance for this specific purpose, against a larger total liability later. Whether that trade is worth making depends on what the avoided near-term borrowing is _for_ — if it's borrowing the state would otherwise have to do anyway, just under worse terms or competing with other capital priorities, smoothing buys breathing room at a real but bounded cost. It is not, on any honest reading, a source of new operating cash for housing, transport or anything else, and this paper should stop describing it that way.

This is also where Fusion's broader position on debt matters: debt is not the villain in this story. Victoria, like every functional government, runs deficits and carries debt as a matter of course. What matters is what the borrowing buys and who gets the return on it. The pension smoothing's contribution to that broader position is indirect: by reducing the near-term debt-issuance the state would otherwise be forced into purely to top up an unfunded super liability, it preserves general government borrowing capacity for the things that actually matter, financing assets the state keeps, housing, transit-linked commercial property, rail infrastructure with a captured land value uplift, rather than that capacity being consumed by a pension catch-up that buys nothing. Borrowing to fund an asset the state keeps is a fundamentally different transaction to borrowing to plug an operating hole or to fund infrastructure whose revenue streams get handed to a private consortium for the next thirty years. The first is leverage. The second is just cost. Every measure in this paper is judged against that test.

One final calibration on where the superannuation mechanism sits within the overall plan: it is a small and supplementary part of a much larger strategy, not its load-bearing pillar. The plan works without it. What the smoothing provides is genuinely useful — reduced near-term debt issuance for this specific purpose, and by extension more headroom in the general government borrowing program for the things this paper argues are worth borrowing for — and it speeds up the creation of seed capital for VWHF's first construction phase. But it is not the only lever available for that seed capital. VWHF's own market-investment mandate generates returns from day one of deployment. The Livret A-style Victorian Savings Bond is a retail deposit channel that doesn't require bond markets at all. The Transit Capital Fund's surcharge revenue provides a third source that requires no borrowing. Any version of this pitch that leads with the pension manoeuvre is leading with the most technically complex, most easily caricatured, and most legally intricate piece of a larger argument whose core logic — build assets the state keeps, capture the value public infrastructure creates, pay Victorians a dividend on the return — stands entirely independently of it.

## Establishing the Victorian Wealth & Housing Fund VWHF

**A Singapore-style wealth fund that builds 30,000 homes Victoria keeps and rents out forever — financed off its own balance sheet, not the state's credit card.**

Fusionʼs core philosophy centers on long-term, future-focused equity. Instead of funding public housing through standard, high-interest government bonds that worsen the state's credit rating, Victoria should create a ring-fenced sovereign wealth vehicle modeled on Singaporeʼs GIC, funded by structural asset reform.

Consolidate the commercial landholdings of VicTrack, Homes Victoria, and the Port of Melbourne into a single entity: the Victorian Wealth & Housing Fund. This fund operates as a non-speculative market actor.

Use the fund to issue Social Infrastructure Bonds, asset-backed against VWHF's commercial portfolio and ring-fenced from general government net debt. The fund uses this capital to build 30,000 mixed-income, rent-stabilized public housing units over five years.

The ongoing rental revenue from the market-rate portion of these developments flows directly back into the fund to service the bonds. Instead of debt-funding standalone estates that draw NIMBY backlash, public housing delivery will be hard-linked to private transit-oriented development.

### How to think about VWHF: a company the public owns

The easiest way to conceptualise VWHF is as a private company. That is not a perfect analogy — VWHF is not a private company, it has no private shareholders, and its purpose is explicitly public — but the way it would operate is closer to a private company than to any other model most people are familiar with, and the distinction matters.

A government department is run by bureaucrats following ministerial direction, with decisions made inside the political cycle, subject to annual budget appropriations, and answerable to a minister who has other priorities. VWHF is not that. A private company is run by professional management accountable to a board, making commercial decisions on timelines that span years or decades, with performance measured against a profit-and-loss statement rather than against a minister's agenda. VWHF is that — except that instead of profits flowing to private shareholders, they flow to Victorians, via the Victorian Dividend and the infrastructure VWHF keeps building.

Singapore's GIC and Temasek are the real-world models that most closely match this structure. Both are wholly state-owned. Both operate with full commercial independence. Both return their earnings to Singapore's sovereign reserves or directly to the government as their sole shareholder. Singapore's ministers do not call GIC to ask it to invest in a politically convenient precinct; GIC's board makes those decisions on the basis of risk-adjusted returns, which is precisely why GIC's balance sheet is worth trusting and why the model has worked for forty-plus years. That independence is the feature, not a side effect.

VWHF's commercial decisions — where to build, what to charge, how to structure a lease, which market investments to make — are made by its board and management, not by the Treasurer's office, not by the Department of Transport and Planning, and not by whoever happens to be Premier at the time. The government sets the mandate in the establishing legislation and appoints the board, the same relationship any government has with an independent statutory authority or a sovereign wealth fund. But it does not run the fund day-to-day. That separation is what prevents VWHF from becoming the thing critics of state enterprise always warn about: a vehicle for politically directed decisions dressed up as commercial ones, and a drain on the budget rather than a contributor to it. The commercial discipline is not a constraint on the public mission. It is the mechanism by which the public mission gets funded.

It is also worth being precise about what VWHF is not: it is not a benevolent enterprise that moderates its profit-seeking out of some sense of social mission, and it should not be run like one. VWHF exists to make a profit, in exactly the same sense any company does, maximising rental yield, turnover-clip revenue, and investment returns is the explicit job of its management, not a target it trades off against softer goals. A state enterprise that asks its managers to sacrifice commercial performance for the sake of "doing good" tends to end up doing neither well, which is the actual lesson of most twentieth-century nationalised industries that failed: not that public ownership is inherently inefficient, but that public ownership without commercial discipline removes the only mechanism that forces an organisation to perform.

What makes VWHF different from an ordinary private company is not a softer mandate. It is who the shareholders are. A private company maximises profit for shareholders who are, for the most part, a different population from its customers and its workforce. VWHF maximises profit for shareholders who are its tenants, its customers, and the broader Victorian public, the same people the fund exists to serve. That single fact means VWHF's profit motive and the public's interest are not in tension; they are the same vector pointed in the same direction. Every dollar VWHF's management extracts through disciplined commercial operation, lower vacancy, higher turnover-clip revenue, a well-run investment portfolio, is a dollar that eventually comes back to a Victorian resident as the Victorian Dividend, a below-market rent, or debt the state doesn't need to backstop. The instruction to VWHF's board should be exactly the instruction any company's board gets: maximise the return to your shareholders. It just happens that, in this case, doing so is the social good.

### The enshittification risk, and why VWHF's structure has to actively guard against it

The clearest cautionary tale for "VWHF precincts should be the most desirable in the state" is the Level Crossing Removal Project's Skyrail corridor. The first stations built under the CD9 corridor came with escalators, lifts, custom architectural finishes and genuinely well-landscaped linear parks underneath the rail line, built that way specifically to win social licence for a politically sensitive program in its early, most contested years. Once the engineering concept was normalised and the program scaled to dozens more level crossings under sustained budget pressure, later stations were value-managed down to bare compliance: pre-cast concrete in place of architectural finish, escalators cut to avoid the lifetime maintenance liability, and the space underneath the rail line left as asphalt and gravel rather than usable public space. The same agency, building the same kind of asset, produced a visibly two-tier network, and it did so through accumulated small decisions rather than one identifiable choice to cut quality.

It's worth being precise about why that happened, because the mechanism is structural, not a matter of bad faith. LXRP was an Administrative Office under the _Public Administration Act 2004_, not a state-owned enterprise and not VWHF's kind of asset-owning commercial entity. It was granted fast-tracked planning powers under the _Major Transport Projects Facilitation Act 2009_ specifically to move quickly and cut through council approvals, and it answered to the Minister for Transport Infrastructure on delivery cost and schedule. Critically, LXRP built and handed over. It never owned the finished asset, never collected a cent of the commercial revenue a well-amenitised station precinct might generate, and never had to live with the consequence of a low-amenity station depressing foot traffic or property value for the next thirty years. Its only enduring KPI was whether the program delivered on budget and on schedule. An escalator is a real, recurring maintenance liability with no offsetting revenue line in that KPI. Cutting it was the economically rational decision for the entity that had to answer for the capital budget and would never answer for the consequence.

VWHF's structure is supposed to break that link, but only because it retains permanent ownership and earns recurring revenue, the turnover clip, rental yield, build-to-sell pricing, that amenity and footfall directly drive. An escalator, decent lighting, and a well-landscaped public realm aren't cost centres to an entity that owns a precinct for decades and collects a share of the turnover happening inside it; they're the things that make the precinct desirable enough to generate that turnover in the first place. That's the same commercial logic that stops Scentre Group letting Chadstone fall into disrepair: a shabby Westfield does worse business. If VWHF's incentives are wired correctly, a shabby VWHF precinct should be a worse investment, not a cheaper one.

"Should" is the operative word, and the LXRP example is exactly why this paper shouldn't rely on the incentive structure working itself out by assumption. The protections need to be specific:

- **Whole-of-life costing as a statutory requirement, not guidance.** VWHF's establishing legislation should require capital allocation decisions on amenity, escalators, landscaping, public realm, to be assessed against their projected effect on footfall, turnover-clip revenue and occupancy over the asset's life, not against headline construction cost alone. LXRP's escalator cuts were "rational" only because the only number on the page was the upfront capital and lifetime maintenance cost; the moment a projected revenue impact sits on the same page, the calculation changes.
- **Design and amenity decisions stay inside the commercial entity that will own the asset.** If VWHF ever needs LXRP-style fast-tracked planning powers to move quickly on its own developments, that power should sit with VWHF itself, not be delegated to an arms-length delivery agency evaluated purely on construction cost and schedule the way LXRP was. The moment the entity making the amenity trade-off is different from the entity that will live with the commercial consequences, LXRP's failure mode reappears regardless of what VWHF's founding documents say about its mission.
- **A rolling amenity floor with mandatory board sign-off for anything below it.** LXRP's drift happened through dozens of individually defensible "value management" decisions, none of which was ever weighed against the network's own established standard, because no such standard existed. VWHF's board should set a rolling baseline, escalator and lift provision, public realm spend per square metre, landscaping standard, benchmarked against its own prior precincts, and any new precinct proposed below that baseline should require an explicit, minuted board decision and a stated commercial justification, not a quiet sign-off three layers down the project hierarchy.
- **Public, comparable reporting across every VWHF precinct.** The same PAEC-style transparency this paper proposes for Southern Cross's CSP and the Cross Yarra Partnership's availability payments should apply here: VWHF should publish a standard amenity scorecard, escalator/lift access, public realm area, landscaping spend, for every precinct it builds, so a two-tier pattern is visible and politically costly the moment it starts, rather than discovered a decade later the way LXRP's was.
- **Management and board incentives tied to long-run portfolio performance, not capex variance.** A team rewarded for hitting a construction budget has the same incentive LXRP's delivery managers had. A team rewarded for occupancy, turnover-clip growth, footfall and tenant retention over a multi-year horizon has a direct personal stake in the amenity that drives those numbers. VWHF's remuneration structure should look like a property fund manager's, not a public works project manager's.

None of this guarantees VWHF never drifts. Westfield itself isn't immune to letting a struggling centre run down. But the structural fix that actually addresses LXRP's failure, ownership plus recurring revenue tied to the thing being cut, has to be paired with these specific, drafted-in mechanisms, or VWHF is simply trusting that good intentions survive contact with a budget blowout in year eight of a thirty-year build-out. LXRP's early stations had exactly those good intentions too.

### The Hong Kong playbook, named explicitly: Rail+Property

Everything above describes VWHF building housing and retail around train stations and keeping the income. That's not a Fusion invention, it's the model behind Hong Kong's MTR Corporation, usually called **Rail+Property (R+P)**, and it's worth naming directly because it's the single most successful transit-finance model on earth, with a forty-year track record rather than a pitch deck.

In simple terms: before a new MTR line opens, the Hong Kong government grants MTR development rights over the land at and around its new stations, priced at _pre-rail_ value, before the line itself makes that land more valuable. MTR then develops that land itself or sells the rights to private developers in exchange for a share of the profit. Because MTR's own railway is what makes that land worth more, MTR captures the value its own infrastructure creates, rather than watching it accrue to whoever happened to own the land next to the station. As of the mid-2010s, roughly 40% of MTR's total revenue came from property rather than fares, which is why MTR has been able to keep fares comparatively low and self-fund network expansion without ongoing government operating subsidy.

VWHF, consolidating VicTrack, Homes Victoria and Port of Melbourne landholdings and building on station-adjacent sites the state already controls, is the Victorian application of exactly this model. Naming it "Rail+Property" rather than leaving it as an unlabelled feature of VWHF does two things. First, it's an instantly recognisable, internationally validated brand: unlike the parametric-reinsurance proposal above, which has no clean precedent and is genuinely first-of-its-kind, R+P doesn't need to be defended from scratch, it can be pointed at MTR's forty-year balance sheet. Second, it sharpens VWHF's mandate: R+P worked because MTR's property arm stayed tied to land its _own_ rail lines made valuable, not because MTR became a generic property developer. The same discipline should apply to VWHF, its property and retail activity should be anchored to station-adjacent sites where VWHF's own infrastructure, or the state's, is what's driving the value, which is also what keeps the "out-competing private developers" framing above honest: VWHF isn't competing with private developers everywhere, just on the land its own transit investment made valuable.

### Beyond property: VWHF as an investor, not just a developer

The Singapore comparison isn't just branding. GIC, the fund VWHF is modelled on, doesn't only develop property — it's one of the world's largest institutional investors, holding a globally diversified portfolio of equities, bonds, infrastructure and alternative assets, precisely so Singapore's reserves aren't a single bet on the domestic property cycle. Nothing about VWHF's structure confines it to Victorian land and buildings either. As an independent, commercially-run entity that returns its profits to its shareholder — the Victorian public, via the Consolidated Fund and the dividend mechanism below — rather than a government department spending against an annual appropriation, there's no reason its legislation needs to wall it off from doing what every comparable wealth fund, future fund, or large superannuation fund already does: once its property portfolio is generating stable rental income and servicing its Social Infrastructure Bonds, surplus capital can be deployed into a broader investment mandate, domestic and international equities, bonds, infrastructure funds, and the proceeds brought home.

This matters for two reasons. First, diversification: a fund whose entire balance sheet is Melbourne transit-precinct real estate is a single bet on one market; spreading surplus capital into broader assets means a downturn in Victorian property doesn't drag down every income stream at once. Second, it's a second answer to the "where does the dividend come from early on" question raised above: a market-investment mandate generates a return on capital from the day it's deployed, independent of construction timelines or rental ramp-up, giving VWHF an income stream that doesn't have to wait on buildings being finished and tenanted.

None of this changes VWHF's core purpose. Building and holding the housing and commercial property described throughout this document remains the primary mandate; the market-investment arm exists to support that mission, not to turn VWHF into a fund that happens to own some car parks on the side. The point is simply that "commercially-run, independent of general government, profits returned to its shareholder" cuts both ways: it's what lets VWHF's bonds be priced on its own portfolio rather than the state's, and it's what lets VWHF put idle surplus to work in the market the same way any other fund its size would, rather than leaving it sitting in a government bank account waiting for the next building to be approved. The shareholder here isn't a private investor expecting a dividend cheque to leave the state — it's every Victorian, which is the entire premise of the Victorian Dividend.

A fair challenge to this structure: an independent ratings agency is unlikely to rate VWHF's bonds above Victoria's own sovereign rating without an explicit state guarantee, and if the state guarantees the debt, agencies like S&P and Moody's will recognise that contingent liability and consolidate it back onto the general government balance sheet, which would undercut the pitch that this is fully "off-book."

That's a fair point, and Fusion doesn't need to win that accounting argument for this to work. If VWHF's bonds land in the A to AA band on the strength of its asset-backed portfolio and ring-fenced revenue, rather than a top-tier rating, that's still a materially better cost of capital than funding 30,000 homes through general government borrowing competing against hospitals and schools for headroom. And if a guarantee turns out to be necessary and the debt gets consolidated onto the state's books, that's acceptable too, for the reason set out above: this is debt that buys an asset the state keeps and earns rent from, which is the kind of borrowing Fusion argues isn't the problem. The point of VWHF was never to hide the debt from the ledger. It's to ring-fence the revenue and the decision-making so housing delivery isn't competing against hospital beds for the same dollar and isn't subject to the same year-to-year political whims as the rest of the capital works program. Whether the debt technically sits on or off the consolidated balance sheet is secondary to whether it's the kind of debt worth having.

A related challenge specific to the sequencing of mechanisms in this paper: if rating agencies observe the state undertaking a superannuation amortisation extension — deferring an existing liability — and read that as balance-sheet stress, doesn't that risk feeding through to VWHF's own bond ratings and undermining the financing model? The answer is that the legal separation between VWHF and the state government is doing real work here, not cosmetic work. The pension manoeuvre operates entirely on the state's general government balance sheet — it adjusts how the Consolidated Fund schedules contributions to the State Superannuation Fund. VWHF's Social Infrastructure Bonds are rated on VWHF's own asset-backed portfolio and its ring-fenced rental revenue. If a ratings agency downgrades the sovereign in response to the pension extension, that affects the state's own borrowing cost; it does not automatically flow through to VWHF's bonds, provided VWHF is demonstrably solvent, its covenants are clean, and its legal separation from the Consolidated Fund is genuine. VWHF is legally distinct for exactly this reason. A well-drafted VWHF establishing act — one that does not allow the state to raid VWHF's assets or guarantee its debts in a way that blurs the legal boundary — is the instrument that keeps these two balance sheets separate in ratings agencies' eyes. Similarly, a commercial real estate downturn that compresses VWHF's portfolio valuations is a risk on VWHF's own books, not on the state's general government credit rating, and vice versa. Getting that legal drafting right is not an afterthought; it is the precondition for the whole structure to work as intended.

### The superannuation capital barrier — and why VWHF doesn't need it

The obvious comparison for VWHF is Singapore's Housing & Development Board, but HDB's financing model leans on something Victoria structurally cannot replicate: the Central Provident Fund, a mandatory national pension scheme from which citizens directly draw down savings to buy HDB flats or service HDB mortgages, with HDB acting as primary mortgagee. In Australia, superannuation is federally regulated under the _Superannuation Industry (Supervision) Act 1993_, and the Victorian Government has no constitutional power to direct private super capital, mandate housing-specific allocations, or let residents draw down their balances to fund a state-retained rental developer. VWHF cannot be "Victoria's CPF-backed HDB," and this paper doesn't pretend otherwise. The Social Infrastructure Bonds described above are the actual financing mechanism, asset-backed direct borrowing against VWHF's own portfolio, not a pension-funded closed loop. The practical consequence is that VWHF's construction phase adds to gross debt issuance before rental yields compound, the same "seed debt" dynamic discussed below for land-banked sites, just at fund level rather than per-site. That's a real constraint on how fast VWHF can scale, not a reason it can't work.

### _Livret A_, named explicitly: where the money for the bonds could come from

"Asset-backed bonds, ring-fenced from general government debt" describes the _structure_ of VWHF's financing, but not who actually buys the bonds. The default answer is the same as for any state bond, institutional investors, super funds, banks, on wholesale markets. France has a second channel worth naming directly, because it points at a retail option Victoria doesn't currently have.

_Livret A_ is a government-regulated, tax-free savings account that any French resident can open at almost any bank, with an interest rate set by the state. A large share of the money deposited in _Livret A_ accounts, roughly 60%, is centralised into the _Fonds d'épargne_, managed by the _Caisse des Dépôts et Consignations_ (CDC), a state financial institution. The CDC then lends that pooled deposit base out at low, stable rates over long terms, overwhelmingly to social housing providers (the HLM sector) and local infrastructure projects. In plain terms: millions of ordinary French households put their everyday savings into an account that pays them a modest, tax-free, government-guaranteed return, and the state channels that pool of cash into building social housing. It's one of the largest sources of low-cost public infrastructure capital in Europe, and the "depositors" are just people with a savings account.

This is the retail mirror image of the Social Infrastructure Bonds, and naming it explicitly suggests a concrete addition: a _Victorian Savings Bond_, a state-backed, tax-advantaged savings account open to any Victorian resident, with deposits pooled and lent directly into VWHF's Social Infrastructure Bond programme (or, combined with lever 2 above, held at a TCV-as-bank and on-lent from there). This doesn't replace wholesale bond-market funding, it sits alongside it as a second, potentially cheaper and more stable source of capital, and it changes the politics of the pitch: instead of "a wealth fund the government runs on your behalf," it becomes "an account you can put your own savings into, that funds the homes and dividend described in this paper." Victoria doesn't have anything like this today, no state-backed retail savings product exists, so unlike Rail+Property above, this would be a genuine first for Australia, though it's a financial product, not a new statutory authority, and could in principle be offered through TCV or any retail bank under contract.

A common shorthand for VWHF's financing — "it's funded by bonds" — understates what's actually on offer and creates a single point of vulnerability in debate: "what happens when bond markets tighten?" The Social Infrastructure Bonds are the largest and most flexible instrument, but they sit alongside two others. The Livret A-style Victorian Savings Bond, just described, draws on a retail deposit channel entirely separate from wholesale capital markets. And VWHF's own market-investment arm — the GIC-style diversification mandate set out in "Beyond property: VWHF as an investor, not just a developer" above — generates returns from the day capital is deployed, independent of construction timelines or any bond issuance at all. The three channels complement each other: if wholesale bond spreads widen, the Victorian Savings Bond fills part of the gap from retail deposits; if the property market softens and rental yields compress, the investment arm's returns provide a floor under VWHF's income. Presenting VWHF's financing as "we issue bonds" misses two thirds of the picture.

Implement a mandatory 20% inclusionary zoning rule for all new multi-unit residential developments within 1 km of any Metropolitan or Regional train station. Half of this allocation 10% transfers directly to Homes Victoria as permanent public housing stock; the other 10% is allocated for long-term affordable rental.

To offset developer costs and unlock supply, these specific zones receive automatic, non-appealable height and density bonuses of up to 50%.

The current Development Facilitation Program lets developers buy out of an equivalent 10% affordable housing requirement with a 3% cash contribution to the Social Housing Growth Fund instead of building the units. That cash-out is exactly the loophole this policy closes, and the criticism that removing it could suppress supply, if the density bonus doesn't fully offset the cost of the foregone market-rate space, deserves a direct answer rather than a footnote.

The 3% cash-out is cheap precisely because it's calibrated to let developers walk away from the physical obligation: it has raised barely $12 million state-wide while doing nothing to put affordable housing in the areas it's meant to serve. The 50% density and height bonus in this proposal is set well above what a 3% buyout would require, because the goal is physical stock in transit-zone buildings, not revenue. Where the bonus doesn't fully cover the cost of the inclusionary units in a given precinct, the fix is to tune the bonus upward for that precinct, monitored against approval volumes, not to reopen a cash-out option that defeats the purpose of the policy. What the state shouldn't do is keep a $12-million-a-year escape hatch that lets developers build "apartments for the rich" with zero change to the housing mix around the station.

If the opposition fights the policy, they are explicitly voting to block private construction, protect land-banking, and deny housing supply during a shortage. If councils fight it, the state exercises its planning powers to bypass them via the Development Facilitation Program.

Victoria already has a windfall gains capture mechanism, and it's a stronger one than this paper needs to invent: the Windfall Gains Tax (WGT), introduced in 2023, taxes the increase in land value triggered by rezoning at up to 50%, with an effective marginal rate of 62.5% in the $100,000–$500,000 taper band. A new policy promising to "capture 25% of the rezoning uplift" would be proposing something weaker than a tax Labor already legislated — a gift to any opponent looking for the line "Fusion's signature land reform is less ambitious than existing law."

So leave the WGT alone and go after what it doesn't touch. The WGT only triggers at the moment of rezoning or sale. It does nothing about land that's already zoned for higher density, sitting within a kilometre of a station the state just spent billions upgrading, where the owner simply declines to develop or sell — because waiting costs them nothing and the land appreciates regardless. That's the actual mechanism by which taxpayer-funded rail lines hand a private windfall to whoever happens to own the surrounding dirt: not a one-off transaction, but an indefinitely deferred one. The Anti-Land-Banking Surface Car Park Tax, set out below, is built specifically to close that gap, and it's the revenue base for the Transit Capital Fund.

The 10% direct transfer of stock to Homes Victoria operates at net-zero capital cost to the state budget. The expenditure is borne by the private development sector, which offsets the cost via the 50% density and height bonuses (yielding more saleable market-rate apartments on the same footprint of land).

## The Transport Efficiency Audit

**$1 billion a year out of privatised rail, trams, buses and tollways — by running out contracts the state already has the leverage to not renew.**

One clarification before the figures, because the opposite is sometimes claimed in debate: public transport fares in Victoria are collected by the state, not by the private operators. Metro Trains Melbourne and Yarra Trams do not keep the farebox revenue — it flows to the Consolidated Fund via Transport Victoria. What the private operators receive is a government payment, an availability and performance fee, calculated under the franchise agreements. The argument for re-municipalisation is not that private operators are "taking the fares," which would be false, and claiming otherwise would be disingenuous. It is that the state is paying a margin on top of the actual cost of operating the network — the private profit, the corporate overhead, and the contract-management cost — that a publicly owned and operated provider would not incur. The saving is in that margin, not in "reclaiming" revenue that was never the operator's in the first place.

The Victorian Auditor-Generalʼs Office’s Managing the Performance of Rail Franchisees report confirms the scale of what the state pays Metro Trains Melbourne and Yarra Trams over their contract lifecycles, though that report audits asset maintenance and oversight, not operator profit margins — no Victorian government audit publicly discloses franchise-specific profit or dividend figures for either operator. Stripping out the built-in private profit margins, operator dividends, separate corporate overheads, and the heavy legal/administrative costs of contract bidding and oversight is assumed, conservatively, to yield a 10% to 15% efficiency dividend on the combined rail and tram payment base, saving roughly $350 million annually across the integrated train and tram networks — see "The honest math" below for exactly what that assumption produces against the operators’ own disclosed payment figures, and where it doesn’t yet add up.

Bus contracts add a fourth, smaller and less-evidenced component. Victoria pays CDC, Dysons, Kinetic and Ventura roughly $1.7 billion across 2023-25, about $850 million a year (see the Bus Plan section above), a figure corroborated independently by VAGO's own "Tendering of Metropolitan Bus Contracts" report, which puts total metropolitan bus contract spend at around $1 billion a year. Applying the same conservative 10% to 15% efficiency dividend used for rail and tram to that base gives $85 million to $127.5 million a year, saving roughly $100 million annually — see "The honest math" below for why this is the least solid of the four numbers in this section.

The 2026-27 State Budget outlines a projected net debt climbing to $199.3 billion by 2029-30, driven heavily by massive capital works. Winding down or pausing non-essential inner and outer-suburban arterial road widening projects — which VAGO's own congestion reports point to as adding car traffic rather than solving congestion — reallocates an estimated $400 million per year in capital expenditure: this paper's own modelled figure, not a number drawn from a specific current budget line; see "The honest math" below.

New York's Mayor Mamdani is a useful live comparison for what agency-level pressure to find efficiency looks like in practice: in January 2026 he established a Chief Savings Officer at every city agency, with savings targets of 1.5% of budget for FY2026 and 2.5% for FY2027, and his administration's official figure, repeated in the May 2026 Executive Budget, claims a combined $1.77 billion in savings across both years. That number is the administration's own self-reported figure, not an independently audited one, at one point only around $200 million of the $1.7 billion target had been verified, with the rest still under review, so it should be read as _claimed_ rather than _confirmed_. Even on that caveated basis it's the right scale of comparison: in Victoria, the equivalent target is the highly fragmented, privatised public transport operator model and the opaque road-building procurement pipeline.

### The honest timeline

To be clear about what kind of mechanism this is: the proposal is to let these contracts run out, not to break them early. Early termination of a PPP triggers compensation clauses that would hand Metro Trains, Yarra Trams or a bus operator a payout for the privilege of leaving, which would torch a large chunk of the saving before it's even banked. Running out the clock costs nothing extra and transfers the asset back to the state at the moment the existing agreement says it should.

That makes the realistic timeline the thing worth being upfront about, because it isn't the same across modes. The Metro Trains Melbourne franchise (MR4) has already been extended twice — most recently to November 2027, largely to align the handover with Metro Tunnel commissioning. That's a genuinely near-term horizon, but only if a third extension doesn't get waved through quietly the way the first two were; a Fusion MP's job in the run-up to 2027 is to make a third extension politically costly, not assumed. Trams are a harder case: in June 2024 the government signed a brand-new nine-year, $6.8 billion contract with the Yarra Journey Makers consortium, running to roughly 2033. That contract is now locked in — there's no near-term "don't renew" lever for trams, full stop.

Buses are a third case again, and a messier one: there's no single contract or single date. Ventura's original metropolitan bus contract runs to 30 June 2028. Kinetic's separate $2.3 billion Melbourne Metropolitan Bus Franchise, covering around 30 per cent of Melbourne's bus services, runs from January 2022 to June 2031. CDC, Dysons and Kinetic each also hold newer ten-year Zero Emission Bus franchise contracts that began 1 July 2025 and run to 30 June 2035. Running out the clock on buses isn't one decision in one year, it's a sequence of decisions stretching from 2028 to 2035, starting with whichever operator's contract is nearest to expiry at the time and treating every renewal after that as a fresh point to apply the same test.

So the $1 billion figure is a destination, reached in stages, not a number that lands in a single budget. The rail component of the $350 million unification saving is realistically a 2027-horizon target. The tram component is a 2033-horizon target, and the work between now and then is making sure that when the Yarra Journey Makers contract approaches expiry, re-municipalisation is the default option on the table rather than a fight that has to be won from scratch. The bus component is the most staggered of all, landing contract-by-contract between 2028 and 2035 rather than on a single date. The capital reallocation and procurement-audit components of the $1 billion don't depend on contract expiry at all and can start immediately. None of this changes the total as a target — it just means the honest framing is "this is where the savings end up once nothing currently locked in gets quietly re-signed," not "this appears in next year's budget."

### The honest math

The $350 million rail-and-tram unification figure deserves the same scrutiny as the timeline above, because the arithmetic needs to be shown, not just asserted. The two payment bases are reasonably well sourced: Metro Trains Melbourne's MR4 franchise is worth roughly $6.3 billion over seven years, near enough $900 million a year, and the Yarra Journey Makers tram contract is $6.8 billion over nine years, about $755.6 million a year. Combined, that's a base of roughly $1.66 billion a year in government payments to the two operators. A "conservative 10% to 15% efficiency dividend" on that combined base is $165.6 million to $248.3 million a year — not $350 million. Reaching $350 million requires closer to 21% of the combined base, a meaningfully more aggressive assumption than "conservative 10-15%" describes.

Worth flagging on its own: MTM's own roughly $900 million a year in government payments (used as half of the combined base above) is close enough to this section's overall $1 billion target that the two are easy to conflate, and they shouldn't be — one is a single operator's entire annual payment from government, the other is a savings target spread across rail, tram and bus unification, road capital reallocation and procurement reform across the whole transport portfolio (and the same $900 million MTM figure is also the one the Victorian Socialists comparison below cites for Metro Trains' annual government payment, which is the correct, unrelated use of it). A hostile reader treating MTM's $900 million payment as if it were itself the saving would be wrong, and it's worth heading off explicitly here rather than leaving the near-miss sitting unexplained.

The one concrete, disclosed data point available on operator profitability cuts against the more aggressive 21% reading, not for it: a 2019 analysis of MTM's financial filings put its actual profit at $29 million on $786 million of revenue, a margin of roughly 3.7%. That figure is old, predates several MR4 contract renegotiations and the post-COVID patronage recovery, and is net profit only, not the broader margin (corporate overhead, contract-bidding and oversight costs, duplicate executive layers) the $350 million figure is trying to capture. But it's the only audited-adjacent number either operator has ever had attached to it publicly, and it sits well under even the low end of "10-15%," let alone 21%. Neither MTM nor Yarra Trams discloses franchise-specific margin data in any VAGO report or parliamentary record, so there is no public document that proves the $350 million figure, and none that disproves it outright either — it sits unverified, between what the strict 10-15%-of-payments math supports ($165.6-248.3 million) and what this paper currently claims ($350 million).

The honest position is to show that gap rather than paper over it. The rigorously defensible number, using only this paper's own cited payment figures and its own stated conservative range, is $165.6 million to $248.3 million a year from rail and tram unification — call it roughly $200 million. The $350 million figure stands as this paper's working assumption that the real capture, once duplicate executive teams, marketing budgets, and the legal/administrative cost of running two separate, audited, litigated contract relationships are added on top of plain profit margin, lands closer to 21% than 10-15%. That's a plausible case, not a proven one. A Fusion MP's actual first move shouldn't be legislating against the $350 million number, it should be commissioning the audit that either confirms it or replaces it with a number backed by primary financial disclosure rather than a modelled assumption.

The bus component added to this section follows the same logic, and arguably needed more caution than rail and tram did. Victoria pays CDC, Dysons, Kinetic and Ventura a combined $1.7 billion across 2023-25 (see the Bus Plan section above), roughly $850 million a year, a figure corroborated independently by VAGO's "Tendering of Metropolitan Bus Contracts" report, which puts total metropolitan bus contract spend at around $1 billion a year. Applying the same conservative 10-15% efficiency dividend used for rail and tram to that base gives $85 million to $127.5 million a year, call it roughly $100 million. But this is the most assumption-heavy figure in the whole table, more so than rail and tram. VAGO has never audited bus operator margins the way it audited Metro Trains and Yarra Trams. The only disclosed proxy available, ComfortDelGro's (CDC's parent company) global operating margin of roughly 4.7%, sits below even the 10% floor, and it's a global figure, not a Victorian one. And the Bus Plan section above found a network VAGO itself describes as more stretched than the department's own data admits, undercounted patronage, declining on-time running, growth-area access gaps, plus bus driver strikes over pay and conditions reported in mid-2025. None of that is what a network with an obvious 10-15% margin sitting idle usually looks like. The $100 million bus figure is included here for the same reason the $400 million and $150 million figures are, because the structural case for not simply renewing these contracts indefinitely is real, but it should be read as the least solid of the four numbers in the table, not the most.

The same caveat applies, with more force, to the remaining two components of the total: the $400 million road-widening reallocation and the $150 million procurement-audit saving. Neither traces to a specific, currently active Victorian budget line, VAGO finding, or Infrastructure Victoria assessment, despite how the table above reads at a glance. The closest real analogue to the road figure is the 2018/19 Suburban Roads Upgrade, a $4 billion package delivered over roughly five years (about $800 million a year at the time) to widen and duplicate a set of named arterials, but that program is historical and largely complete, not a current line that could be paused today. VAGO's reports on arterial congestion and on major-project cost overruns (cited in the bibliography) support the underlying logic, induced demand from road widening is a real, well-evidenced effect, and Victoria's major transport projects ran a combined $3.8 billion over budget across 117 projects between 2019-20 and 2021-22, but neither report produces a $400 million or a $150 million figure specifically. Both numbers should be read as this paper's own modelled estimates of plausible scale, in the same spirit as the transport-rebranding cost estimate elsewhere in this document, which is explicitly flagged as a rough industry figure rather than an audited one, not as cited findings.

None of that makes the $1 billion destination wrong as a target. It does mean roughly $650 million of it ($400 million road, $150 million procurement, $100 million bus) currently rests on no specific public source at all, and the remaining $350 million rests on an assumed operator margin more aggressive than this paper's own "conservative" framing claims, one the single disclosed real-world data point available doesn't support. What survives this stress test intact is the structural argument: running franchises out rather than renewing them, and reallocating capital away from demand-inducing road widening, are real, available levers. What doesn't yet survive it is the precision of $1 billion as an already-audited number. Tightening that, with a real Auditor-General review of operator margins across all three modes and a genuine costing of procurement-audit savings, rather than this paper's own modelling, is exactly the work that should happen before this figure goes anywhere near an actual budget bill.

### Availability payments and the incentive problem: renegotiate regardless

The run-out-the-clock strategy above addresses when these contracts end. A separate, more immediate problem is how they pay — because the availability-based structure that governs both the Metro Trains MR4 franchise and the Cross Yarra Partnership's concession is misaligning incentives right now, independently of whether either contract is eventually wound up or renegotiated wholesale.

Under an availability-payment model, the private operator receives a flat fee so long as the asset is "available" — trains running, stations open, basic service delivered to the contractual threshold — regardless of how many passengers actually ride, whether ridership is growing or falling, or whether the service is genuinely good or merely adequate. Running an excellent service and running a barely-compliant one pay exactly the same. That structure removes any financial reason for the operator to do more than meet the contractual floor. It is the structural opposite of the "Westfield clip" described elsewhere in this paper, where the landlord's income rises with the tenant's success. In transit, it produces operators who optimise for contract compliance rather than passenger experience, because contract compliance is what they are paid for.

This is worth renegotiating regardless of what ultimately happens to these contracts. Whether Metro Trains MR4 is wound up in 2027 or extended again, and whether the Cross Yarra Partnership's concession runs to 2050 or is restructured earlier, the availability-only incentive structure is a problem that exists on its own terms today. A renegotiation that converts a material share of the flat availability fee into a performance-linked component — tied to patronage growth above a baseline, on-time performance above a meaningful threshold, or independently measured customer satisfaction — doesn't require waiting for a contract expiry. It requires the state to use exactly the kind of proactive renegotiation leverage the PPP Renegotiation Unit in the Seven More Levers section is designed to apply. The goal isn't necessarily to get the fee lower; it's to make the operator's financial interest and the state's interest point in the same direction. An operator that earns more when more people ride the train is an operator with a reason to care whether the trains run well.

## The Rebrand Merry-Go-Round: Changing the Logo Instead of Running the Trains

**Victoria's public transport has been renamed at least six times since 1983. Since PTV was created in 2012 as a genuine structural reform, every subsequent rebrand has been bureaucratic theatre — and the government has never told anyone what any of it cost.**

Melbourne's transport branding history reads like a government in perpetual motion while the actual network stays still. The full sequence since 1983: **The Met** → privatisation chaos (Hillside Trains, Bayside Trains, Connex, M>Train, Swanston Trams, etc.) → **Metlink** umbrella brand (2003) → **PTV / Public Transport Victoria** (2012) → absorbed into the **Department of Transport** (2019, but the PTV brand quietly kept) → new **DTP logo rollout** (2021) → **Transport Victoria** (2024–25). Transport blogger Daniel Bowen titled a post in 2014 _"Rebranding number 6 (in 20 years): Can we stick with 'PTV' for a while please?"_ He was counting the vehicle livery changes alone.

### The one that was actually justified

_(A note on political honesty: what follows credits the Baillieu Liberal government with doing something right. That's deliberate. Fusion doesn't have friends or enemies among the major or minor parties — we have agreements and disagreements, issue by issue. Good policy gets credited regardless of who implemented it; bad policy gets called out regardless of who is responsible. The rest of this document applies exactly that standard to the Andrews and Allan Labor governments. We're not a "Labor but more so" party, or a "Greens but different" party, or a protest vote against anyone in particular. The non-tribal position is the actual position.)_

The creation of PTV on 2 April 2012 under the Baillieu government was a genuine structural reform, not just a rebadge. It merged Metlink (the marketing-only umbrella body), the Public Transport Division of the Department of Transport, and the Transport Ticketing Authority (which ran myki) into a single statutory authority with real planning and contracting powers. The ~80,000 signs changed across 28,000 bus stops, 4,000 tram stops and 350 stations; buses were relivered in a unified orange over two-plus years; trains and trams followed. That physical change was the visible output of a real organisational consolidation. The cost was never disclosed, but the structural change was real.

The current **PTV → Transport Victoria** rebrand also has a defensible logic: the Department of Transport and Planning has absorbed VicRoads functions into a single customer-facing platform at transport.vic.gov.au, so bringing public transport and roads information under one brand genuinely reduces fragmentation for users. The PTV website was also approaching end-of-life and required reinvestment regardless. Reasonable people can argue this one was worth doing.

### Everything in between was not

**2019 — The invisible abolition.** On 1 July 2019, the Andrews government dissolved PTV as a statutory authority and absorbed it into the new Department of Transport. A separate public body, which published its own annual reports and performance data, simply ceased to exist. And yet: nothing visible changed. The PTV brand, the PTV website, the PTV app, the PTV signs — all stayed in place. The organisation was restructured away in secret while its public face remained entirely intact. Commuters were given no indication that the body they were dealing with no longer legally existed. The practical effect was to eliminate PTV's independent accountability, its standalone annual reports and performance metrics quietly folded into a departmental aggregate, without even having to announce the decision. No rebranding cost, because no one changed any signs. No transparency cost, because no one noticed.

**2021 — Pausing improvements to change the logo.** After the 2019 administrative merger, the Department of Transport and Planning eventually developed new combined branding for its "Transport for Victoria" family of agencies. Implementing it required network-wide sign replacement and vehicle livery changes. According to the Public Transport Users Association, investment in upgrades was **paused for twelve months from April 2021** specifically to prioritise replacing signage and liveries with the new logo. Read that again: the department suspended actual service and infrastructure improvements for a year in order to change logos. The cost of this rollout was, once again, never publicly disclosed.

### The cost nobody will name

Across every one of these transitions, the Victorian government has maintained a perfect record: **it has never publicly disclosed the total cost of a single transport rebrand.** Not Metlink to PTV, not the 2021 logo refresh, not the current PTV to Transport Victoria rollout. No Victorian Auditor-General has specifically audited transport rebranding expenditure. No freedom-of-information request appears to have forced the figures out. No parliamentary question has produced a line-item answer.

The physical scope makes this omission striking. A full rebrand of the Melbourne and regional Victoria transport network touches roughly 80,000 signs, hundreds of buses (Ventura Bus Lines alone had 494 vehicles relivered over two years for the PTV rollout), dozens of train sets, the tram fleet, myki cards, ticketing machines, the website, the journey-planner app, and all printed collateral. Even the most conservative estimate — $20–30 per sign panel, $3,000–$15,000 per bus repaint, proportional costs for trains and trams — puts vehicle livery alone in the tens of millions per full cycle. A realistic total for each full physical rebrand (Metlink→PTV, PTV→TV) sits somewhere in the **$30–100 million range**, compounded across multiple cycles. The government doesn't know? Or it knows and doesn't want to say?

### What this is actually about

Rebranding is what a transport bureaucracy does when it can't point to service improvements. A new logo is an announcement you can make regardless of whether trains are running on time, whether the 40-minute Sunday morning timetable on half the Metro network has been fixed, or whether growth-area residents have a bus that connects to the station that was built near them but not for them. It generates press releases and renders on social media. It doesn't require fixing anything.

The pattern since 2012 is consistent: each time a new government or a new minister wants to signal that transport is being taken seriously, the answer has been a restructure and a rebrand rather than a service improvement. The Baillieu government's PTV creation was the exception — it came with actual new powers and actual consolidation of previously fragmented functions. The Andrews government's 2019 abolition of PTV as a standalone body came with none of that; it made the organisation less accountable and the commuter no better served, while the brand stayed in place to absorb any complaints as if nothing had changed. The 2021 logo refresh consumed a year of improvement capacity.

A Fusion MP's ask here is simple and hard to refuse: **table the total cost of each rebrand since 2012, on the record, at PAEC**. Not "what was the design agency fee" — the total, including sign fabrication and installation, vehicle repainting, digital asset rebuild, printed materials, and staff time. If the government can't produce that number, the Auditor-General should be asked to find it. Victorians have paid for these rebrands multiple times over. They're entitled to know how much.

This pattern isn't confined to logos. Victoria's Bus Plan, the government's flagship 2021 commitment to rebuild the bus network into a genuine mass-transit option, ran into the same instinct: when the Legislative Council ordered the Bus Plan's internal documents released in March 2024, the government claimed executive privilege over the action plan itself, the document setting out what was promised against what's actually been delivered. A renamed network and a withheld delivery plan are the same move aimed at different audiences. The bus case has its own Auditor-General's report attached, tabled in June 2026, and it's detailed enough to warrant its own section, next.

## The Bus Plan: A 2031 Promise the Auditor-General Says Won't Be Kept

**VAGO's June 2026 audit of Victoria's Bus Plan found the network won't hit its 2031 "mass transit" target on current trajectory, that the department's own punctuality figures sit on a narrower measure than what passengers experience, and that growth-area communities the plan was supposed to help are the ones it's furthest behind on.**

The Bus Plan was announced in 2021 with six reform objectives: a simpler, faster, more reliable network; a cleaner, zero-emissions fleet from 2025; better-performing buses through priority infrastructure; a better customer experience; better governance and data; and value for money under existing and new operator contracts. The department was meant to spend 2021-23 planning the reform and 2023-30 delivering it, with the network becoming a genuine mass-transit option from 2031. None of that is a small commitment: Victoria pays four private operators, CDC, Dysons, Kinetic and Ventura, around $1.7 billion over 2023-25 to run 366 routes covering 6,000 kilometres, carrying 114.9 million passenger trips in 2024-25, a quarter of all public transport trips in the state.

The Victorian Auditor-General's Office audited the Bus Plan's progress and tabled its findings on 17 June 2026. The conclusion: on present indications, the department will not deliver the Bus Plan's mass-transit ambition. Implementing it through routine planning and management, which is what's been happening since 2021, won't get there; several of the action plan's most consequential items, network design, planning guidance, the infrastructure investment pipeline, need a government decision the department hasn't been given.

### The numbers behind "won't deliver"

Two separate failures sit underneath that conclusion, and they cut in opposite directions, which is worth being precise about.

First, the department's own performance reporting makes the network look better than it is. Its punctuality measure only checks selected "timing points," the major stops along a route, not every stop a passenger actually waits at, and on that measure 86 per cent of services ran on time against an 86 per cent target in 2024-25, a result the department reports as meeting target. VAGO checked every stop on every route instead and found on-time running at around 70 per cent, having declined from 75 per cent in 2021 and stabilised at that lower level for the last two years. A punctuality target that's only ever tested against a subset of stops is a target that's structurally easier to hit than the one passengers actually experience.

Second, the department's demand modelling makes the network look less used than it actually is. Bus operators carried 114.9 million passengers in 2024-25, missing the department's own target of 125.3 million for a fifth straight year, a shortfall that reads as soft demand. But the department estimates ridership from myki tap-on data run through a "boost factor" correction for passengers who don't scan, and VAGO tested that method against buses fitted with automated passenger counters. The actual count came in 25.7 per cent higher on average per trip than the department's modelled figure, and VAGO's analysis suggests the department's method may be undercounting the metropolitan network by up to 30 million trips a year. The same audit that shows buses running less punctually than reported also shows them carrying more people than reported. Both numbers point the same way: the department's data understates how stretched the network already is, in the direction that should make the case for investment stronger, not weaker.

Bus-to-train transfers fare similarly: VAGO found that, in June 2025, only 68-73 per cent of priority bus services connected to a train within a workable 5-10 minute window, varying by direction, day and time of day, despite "buses turning up reliably and on time" ranking as commuters' second-highest priority in the department's own consultations, just behind frequency. The department has a coordination methodology but no consistent target for it.

### The access numbers, and what they confirm about Melton

VAGO also measured how close Melbourne households actually are to a usable bus service, and the results put hard figures on a pattern this paper has argued from elsewhere. Citywide, 87 per cent of households sit within 400 metres of some bus service, but only 26 per cent are within 800 metres of a _high quality_ one, defined as running at least every 20 minutes from 6am to 8pm on weekdays, until at least 9pm, and on both weekend days. Folding in trains and trams lifts that to 37 per cent. Most households can get to _a_ bus stop. Most can't get to a bus stop worth building a commute around.

The gap is worst exactly where this paper has already argued the state has under-invested: outer metropolitan growth areas that don't have train or tram coverage to fall back on. Cardinia has less than 1 per cent of its population within reach of high-quality public transport of any kind. Casey and Melton sit at 9-12 per cent. Hume and Whittlesea, somewhat better, at 21-24 per cent. Inside those LGAs, the high-population growth pockets are worse again: Rockbank–Mt Cottrell, inside Melton, has no high-quality public transport within 800 metres at all, and less than a quarter of residents can reach even an ordinary bus stop within 400 metres. Mickleham–Yuroke (Hume) and Beaconsfield–Officer (Cardinia) do better on basic access, around half the population within 400 metres of any bus, but high-quality service remains close to non-existent in both.

Melton is the same LGA the Woodgrove transition-mechanics worked example above is built around, and this is the Auditor-General's confirmation, not just this paper's anecdote, that the bus network underneath that worked example is as structurally inadequate as claimed. It's also the reason that worked example insists on sequencing a dedicated bus rapid transit spine into Woodgrove _before_ a single car park space comes out: VAGO's numbers are what "the bus has to land first" is actually protecting against.

### The transparency failure, again

This is the same instinct the Rebrand Merry-Go-Round section above describes, just aimed at a delivery plan instead of a logo. The department consulted communities on bus reform pilots through Engage Victoria in September-October 2022 and has not publicly updated anyone on the outcome since. In March 2024, the Legislative Council ordered the Bus Plan's implementation documents released. The department finally produced some of them in September 2025, three years after the consultation closed, but withheld the Bus Reform Implementation Plan itself, the actual document setting out what was promised and what's been delivered, under a claim of executive privilege. VAGO's own description of this is about as pointed as an Auditor-General's office gets: it was "another opportunity for the department to explain the lack of implementation progress, but it did not take it up."

The result is three recommendations, all to the same department, on data quality, on setting and measuring against a household-access standard, and on publicly reporting progress, and the department has accepted them only "in part" or "in principle." That's not a rejection. It's also not a commitment to a number or a date, which is the same soft landing the rebrand section above documents the department reaching for every time scrutiny shows up without an enforceable deadline attached.

### The honest framing

This was a reasonable-assurance performance audit, VAGO's higher-confidence tier, built on evidence gathered and tested independently rather than the lower-confidence "assurance review" that relies mainly on an agency's own representations. The findings carry the weight that comes with that distinction.

It's also worth being precise about what this audit didn't find, because overstating it would repeat the mistake this paper has already corrected elsewhere. VAGO didn't examine bus operator contract pricing or profit margins the way its rail franchise reports examined Metro Trains and Yarra Trams. The Transport Efficiency Audit's $100 million bus component (see "The honest math" in that section) is this paper's own modelled estimate applied to the same $1.7 billion paid to CDC, Dysons, Kinetic and Ventura over 2023-25 cited above, not a figure this audit produced. If anything, this audit's findings argue for caution on that number specifically: a network VAGO shows is already running more punctually-challenged and more patronage-undercounted than the department's own data admits is not an obvious place to assume a comfortable margin is sitting idle, waiting to be stripped out. This audit's contribution is narrower and different in kind regardless: not "the contracts cost too much," but "the department doesn't reliably know whether the network works, and hasn't told anyone when it found out it might not hit its own target."

It's also fair to credit the department with real, if insufficient, progress: more than 128 local network upgrades since 2021 adding over 11,000 weekly services, and FlexiRide on-demand services reaching growth areas like Tarneit North and Melton South that don't yet have fixed routes. VAGO credits this work. Its finding is that incremental change at this pace cannot deliver the scale of reform the Bus Plan itself describes, not that nothing has happened.

### The PAEC ask

VAGO's three recommendations already write the question. A Fusion MP's PAEC ask is to put a number and a date against each one: what is the department's aspirational standard for household proximity to high-quality bus services, and what is today's measured gap against it; when will the data-quality fixes to punctuality and patronage modelling actually land; and will the department release the Bus Reform Implementation Plan in full, the same document withheld behind executive privilege despite a standing Legislative Council order, rather than continuing to accept its own recommendations only "in principle." The audit itself cost $720,000 and took eleven months, a rounding error against the $1.7 billion the state pays bus operators over the same period it covers. Scrutiny here is cheap. The department's reluctance to volunteer it isn't a budget problem, it's the same instinct documented throughout this section, and the rest of this paper.

## Asset Re-municipalisation Case Study: Southern Cross Station

**When Civic Nexus's contract lapses, Victoria takes back Southern Cross's commercial revenue for $0 extra — at no new cost to the budget.**

The financial data for Southern Cross Station shows that waiting out the Civic Nexus contract delivers a real budget benefit, though the precise size depends on figures the government has never been forced to put on the record. According to financial industry profiles, the operational arm “Southern Cross Station Pty Ltd” pulled in $93.37 million in total revenue for 2025.

If the state lets the 30-year Services and Development Agreement “SDA” expire naturally in the mid-2030s, the budget effect runs on two fronts: ending a CPI-indexed annual payment to Civic Nexus, and capturing the high-volume commercial revenue stream the consortium currently keeps. Against that, the state takes on direct responsibility for the station's maintenance and operating costs, which Civic Nexus currently bundles into its fee.

### What the state currently pays

The public assumes Southern Cross runs at no cost to the taxpayer because it is a PPP. In reality, the original Services and Development Agreement requires the state to make ongoing quarterly Core Service Payments “CSPs” to Civic Nexus, covering the historical construction debt, building insurance, and station maintenance. The CSP was set at roughly $30 million a year when the contract was signed in the early 2000s — but it is contractually indexed to CPI. Compounded over the two-plus decades since signing, the figure the Department of Transport and Planning actually pays today is more plausibly in the **$60-70 million a year** range, not the $30-35 million commonly quoted. If the true figure is closer to $60-70M, the saving from letting the contract lapse is correspondingly larger than the headline number this document previously used.

### What the state would pick up

Letting the SDA expire doesn't make the maintenance and operating cost disappear — it shifts who pays it. The state (or a body acting on its behalf) becomes responsible for cleaning, security, building services and ongoing structural maintenance directly. Realistic estimates of that standalone opex are likely less than the $60-70 million CPI-indexed CSP, because the CSP also amortises historical construction debt and insurance costs that don't recur once the asset sits debt-free on the state's books. The gap between the CSP the state stops paying and the opex it starts paying is the net saving on this side of the ledger.

### What offsets the remaining cost

Financial analysis by fixed-income watchdogs reveals that Civic Nexus draws roughly 30% of its total revenue from non-government commercial sources — generated by the 6 million commuters passing through the gates each month:

- Retail lease tenancies: ~$15 Million

- Commuter car parking & airport bus terminal access fees: ~$10 Million

- Exclusive out-of-home digital advertising rights “JCDecaux”: ~$3 Million

- Total Captured Commercial Revenue: ~$28 Million / year

By bringing the facility back into public hands, the state captures this commercial stream as new revenue — most naturally as new annual recurring revenue (ARR) for VWHF — which further offsets whatever net operating cost remains.

### The honest framing

A previous version of this document presented this as a clean "$32M saved + $28M captured = $60 million annual swing directly to the bottom line." That overstates the case in one direction — the CSP saving is likely closer to $60-70M once CPI indexation is properly accounted for — and understates it in the other, because standalone opex doesn't vanish, it transfers to whoever runs the station.

The defensible version of this case is narrower but still strong: hand Southern Cross's operations to VWHF at contract expiry with no net increase in general government spending outlay. The avoided CSP and the captured ~$28M in commercial ARR between them comfortably cover the station's standalone running costs, and any genuine surplus flows to VWHF rather than to Civic Nexus's shareholders. The precise size of that surplus depends on numbers — the actual current indexed CSP, and a real estimate of Southern Cross's standalone opex — that only PAEC can force into the public record (see the PAEC mechanism below), so the specifics here should be treated as to be confirmed rather than as a settled figure.

It still reframes the political landscape:

The major parties want to spend public money to build assets, then let private consortia pocket the retail rent, car park fees, and an indexed availability fee that's grown well past what was originally announced.

Fusion wants to let the contracts expire naturally, take back the keys for $0, hand operations to VWHF, and keep the commercial upside in public hands — without it costing the budget a cent more than it already does.

## The Metro Tunnel and Cross Yarra Partnership: The Next Southern Cross, Signed Before It Opened

**The Metro Tunnel's five new stations are already on track to become the next Southern Cross. Lock their retail precincts into VWHF now, while the leases are still being signed.**

Everything in the Southern Cross case study above describes a contract from the early 2000s reaching the end of its life — a one-off chance for the state to claw back revenue once a decades-old PPP finally lapses. The Metro Tunnel is the same playbook, mid-signature, for the next thirty years, except there's no "wait for it to expire" lever here. It only opened in 2026, and the clock just started.

The Tunnel and Stations component of the Metro Tunnel, the twin 9km rail tunnels under the CBD and the five new underground stations at Arden, Parkville, State Library, Town Hall and Anzac, was delivered under a Public Private Partnership with the Cross Yarra Partnership (CYP), a consortium of Lendlease, John Holland, Bouygues Construction and Capella Capital, reaching financial close in December 2017. Structurally, it's an availability PPP: CYP designed, built and financed the asset, and now, for a 25-year concession period from the asset's opening, receives quarterly availability payments from the state regardless of how many people actually ride the trains. Patronage risk sits with government; guaranteed revenue sits with CYP. That's the same shape as Civic Nexus at Southern Cross and the rail and tram franchises covered above — except this one's 25-year clock runs to roughly 2051, not a date already in the rear-view mirror.

### The business case's own numbers undercut it

The 2016 business case justified going down the PPP path over conventional public procurement on the basis of a projected value-for-money saving against the public sector comparator of around $85 million — over a 10-year horizon, against a project that was, even then, a roughly $11 billion commitment. Even taken at face value, that's a saving of well under a tenth of one percent a year on the project's own headline cost — the kind of figure that's well within the margin of error of the modelling that produced it, never mind the billions in subsequent variations. A Fusion MP doesn't need to argue the PPP was the wrong call in hindsight; the business case's own published rationale for choosing it is smaller than a single rounding adjustment in any of the cost blowouts that followed. If $85 million over 10 years was the entire premium the state was paying for "risk transfer," and the state then paid out well over $2 billion in additional construction-phase contributions anyway, the "risk transfer" didn't just fail to materialise — it was never worth enough to justify the structure in the first place.

### The cost blowout, and who actually paid for it

The Metro Tunnel was announced in 2016 at a total project cost of around $11 billion. By September 2024, the government's own figures put the total at $13.48 billion, an increase of roughly $2.5 billion, and some reporting around the project's 2025-26 opening cites figures as high as $15.5 billion for the project overall — it's unclear whether that higher number reflects a different scope (the state-funded budget line versus a total cost including CYP's own financing and contributions), and that gap is exactly the kind of reconciliation PAEC should be made to force into the open, the same way this paper treats the Southern Cross CSP figure as "to be confirmed" rather than settled. What isn't in dispute is the direction: this was sold as a fixed-price PPP where the private partner carries construction and schedule risk, and it overran anyway, by billions.

What is in dispute is the framing that taxpayers were protected from that overrun. The Victorian Auditor-General's Office's review of the project's main works documents a contract "reset" after CYP's own cost-overrun claims were mediated through an independent process, and in December 2020 the government signed amending and settlement deeds under which it agreed to contribute up to an extra $1.37 billion towards construction-phase costs, on top of the original contract price, conditional on CYP hitting delivery milestones. In 2024, as the opening date slipped again, reporting (including from the parliamentary opposition, so the precise figure should be treated as contested pending PAEC confirmation) put a further round of additional payments to CYP at up to $888 million, tied to the 2025 opening target. Strip away the PPP branding and the actual sequence is: the private consortium hit cost and schedule problems, and the state picked up a large share of the bill anyway, while CYP's 25-year availability payment stream, sized against the post-reset contract value, locks in regardless of how the construction phase actually went.

### The delays

The original 2025 opening — itself already a slip from an earlier internal target of September 2024 — became a "soft" preview opening on 30 November 2025, with full timetabled service from 1 February 2026. Each slip is also, mechanically, a trigger for the kind of variation claims described above: every month CYP doesn't hand over a working asset is another month the state is negotiating, again, over who pays for the gap.

### Why this matters for the rest of this paper

The 25-year availability payment clock on the Metro Tunnel starts now, in 2026. There is no expiry-based remunicipalisation lever here the way there eventually will be for MR4 or Yarra Trams — this is closer to the _start_ of a Civic Nexus-style arrangement than the end of one, and the next chance to renegotiate who captures the upside is a generation away. What isn't a generation away is the commercial space inside five brand-new underground stations sitting on some of the most valuable transit-adjacent land in the state. State Library and Town Hall stations alone sit in the densest commercial precincts in the CBD. Nothing in the CYP construction PPP requires the retail tenancies in those stations to be leased out the way Civic Nexus runs Southern Cross's, and every square metre of station retail that defaults to the VWHF model instead is a square metre that doesn't need a 2050s PAEC fight to bring home. The fight worth having now isn't about the tunnel's construction contract — that's signed, settled and sunk. It's about making sure the commercial precincts in and around these five stations go to VWHF from day one, rather than quietly becoming the next thirty-year leasing arrangement somebody else has to claw back.

### The PAEC ask

The same mechanism proposed for Southern Cross applies here, sooner. The exact size of CYP's availability payments, the post-reset contract value they're calculated against, and the commercial leasing arrangements (if any) for retail space across the five new stations have never been put on the public record as a single, reconciled set of figures. A Fusion MP's PAEC questions on the Metro Tunnel shouldn't wait for a contract expiry that's decades away — they should start now, while the numbers are fresh and the retail leases for the new stations are still being signed.

### Correction: the retail precincts are already someone else's, by contract, until 2050

An earlier framing of this section treated the five new stations' retail space as up for grabs, "lock it into VWHF before it gets leased away." That's wrong, and it's worth being precise about why, because the correction changes the recommendation. The CYP Project Agreement grants the consortium the exclusive right to manage and capture commercial and retail opportunities, up to 30 retail shops, station advertising, vending and brand activations, across all five stations for a 25-year operational term running to roughly 2050. Construction is complete and operations have commenced: this isn't a pipeline contract that hasn't been signed yet, it's an active one. Under the _Partnerships Victoria_ framework, the state can't unilaterally claw back or relet that retail space to VWHF without a material breach, and a "State Modification" triggers the standard compensation clauses: the "no worse off" principle, under which the state has to pay CYP the net present value of the 25 years of retail rent and advertising profit the variation takes away. That NPV payout would likely exceed whatever rental yield VWHF could extract from the same space, funded upfront by the taxpayer, for a net loss, on top of the reputational and credit-rating cost of a government unilaterally varying an active PPP. This is structurally the same mistake as early-terminating Metro and Yarra Trams, discussed above: the saving on paper doesn't survive the exit cost.

So the fix here isn't reclamation, it's value capture around the asset rather than inside it. The five stations sit on some of the most valuable rezoned land in the CBD and inner north, State Library and Town Hall alone are in the densest commercial precincts in the state, and every one of them is a Windfall Gains Tax and Infrastructure Contributions Plan trigger point regardless of what CYP does inside the station footprint. Direct the WGT uplift and ICP levies collected on the development sites _around_ each of the five stations straight into VWHF, the same mechanism proposed for the broader Transit Capital Fund. That captures the land-value half of "the next Southern Cross" without touching CYP's contractual retail rights at all, and it's available now, while those surrounding sites are still being rezoned and developed, not in 2050. The retail-reclamation ask for these five stations should wait until the actual expiry approaches, the same PAEC-driven, expiry-clock-leverage approach this paper applies to Southern Cross, MR4 and the tram contract, not be attempted while the agreement is still live.

## Scoping the Pattern: How Many Stations Actually Look Like Southern Cross?

**Six stations out of roughly 309 run on the Civic Nexus/Cross Yarra model. That's the honest headcount — but the revenue those six stations capture rivals what the entire rest of the state-owned network earns combined, which cuts against the obvious follow-up question rather than confirming it.**

This section exists because the obvious objection to everything above deserves a direct answer, not a hand-wave: if Southern Cross and the Metro Tunnel are the only two examples of a privately-run station, how much of this paper's argument is actually riding on two contracts out of a network with hundreds of stations? It's worth separating two different claims this paper makes, because they get conflated easily and shouldn't be. The Transport Efficiency Audit's $1 billion figure is about train, tram and bus _operating_ franchises, Metro Trains, Yarra Trams and the metropolitan bus operators running the actual vehicles, which covers the entire metropolitan network. The Southern Cross and Metro Tunnel case studies are about something narrower: who captures the _commercial_ revenue, retail rent, car parking, advertising, generated inside a station building, separate from running the trains through it. On that narrower question, here's the scope check.

### The headcount

Victoria's passenger rail network runs to roughly 227 Metro Trains Melbourne stations (including the five new Metro Tunnel stations) and around 82 V/Line regional stations, close to 309 stations in total. Of those, exactly six, Southern Cross and the five Metro Tunnel stations, are run under a contract handing a private consortium multi-decade rights to capture a station's commercial revenue. Checked against the network's other major delivery vehicles, the Suburban Rail Loop East's TransitLinX consortium holds a 15-year operations-and-maintenance contract, shorter than Cross Yarra Partnership's 25-year concession, with no retail or commercial rights identified anywhere in its public scope; the Mernda extension, the Cranbourne-Pakenham duplication, and the various Level Crossing Removal Project stations were all delivered by construction alliances (Lendlease, McConnell Dowell, John Holland and others) that dissolve once the build is finished, with ownership and commercial leasing reverting to VicTrack, not a retained consortium. A 2019 assessment from the transport-commentary site Melbourne on Transit put it bluntly, before the Metro Tunnel even opened: Southern Cross was "alone among stations in being run by a private consortium, unconnected with Metro Trains." Add the five Metro Tunnel stations and that's still an accurate description of the other 303.

For everything outside those six, the institutional arrangement is the same one across most of the network: VicTrack owns the land and station buildings, leases the operating infrastructure to the state, which sub-leases it to Metro Trains Melbourne (MTM) or V/Line under their respective franchise agreements, and VicTrack itself, not MTM, remains landlord of record for retail tenancies, car parks and advertising space. The Victorian Auditor-General's 2016 review of rail franchisee performance confirms MTM's franchise lets it earn its own commercial revenue too, but only from advertising and renting out surplus land or buildings, a narrower, secondary entitlement layered on top of an operations contract, not a dedicated 25-to-30-year retail concession of the Civic Nexus or Cross Yarra type.

### The revenue scale doesn't follow the headcount

That headcount is good news for the first half of the concern this section opened with: the claw-back mechanism really does only apply to six stations, not the network. It's also exactly where the concern needs sharpening rather than dropping, because revenue doesn't track headcount. Civic Nexus's own commercial take at Southern Cross, retail leases (~$15M), car parking and airport bus terminal fees (~$10M), and advertising rights (~$3M), the same breakdown used in the case study above, totals around $28 million a year on its own. That figure deliberately excludes the roughly $60-70 million CPI-indexed CSP the state pays Civic Nexus, which is a government payment recycled through the contract, not new economic activity, the same distinction the Southern Cross case study draws when it warns against quoting Southern Cross Station Pty Ltd's full $93.37 million revenue figure as if it were all commercial income.

VicTrack, meanwhile, reported $42.5 million in property-related income for 2023-24 and $69.77 million for 2024-25 across the entire rest of the network, every other metro and regional station, plus the rest of its landholdings as Victoria's fourth-largest landowner. That jump mostly reflects an accounting reclassification, land-sale proceeds moved into the property-income line rather than sitting in "other income", rather than a genuine surge in leasing activity; restated on the same basis, the prior year would have been $68.2 million, a modest, single-digit increase. Take either figure, and the comparison holds: one station's private commercial operator captures somewhere between 40 and 65 cents for every dollar VicTrack earns in property income from everything else it owns across the state, hundreds of other stations included. That's not evidence the privatised pattern is a rounding error against a thriving publicly-run network. It's evidence of how little commercial activity the other ~300 stations currently generate, full stop, regardless of who runs them.

### Revenue per passenger, to control for the fact Southern Cross is also just busier

A fair objection to the comparison above is that Southern Cross might simply earn more because more people pass through it, not because it's better activated. Normalising for traffic answers that directly. Southern Cross recorded 20.16 million station entries in 2023-24 (14.02 million metropolitan, 5.93 million V/Line regional, per Victorian Government open patronage data), and that figure is rail-gate entries only, it excludes SkyBus terminal passengers and interstate/intrastate coach passengers who use the building without crossing a rail fare gate, so it understates the building's actual commercial foot traffic rather than overstating it. Against that figure, Civic Nexus's ~$28 million in commercial-only revenue works out to roughly $1.39 per passenger entry.

The rest of the network, on the same basis: Metro Trains Melbourne and V/Line carried a combined 206.3 million entries in 2023-24 (182.5 million metro, 23.8 million V/Line); subtracting Southern Cross's own 20.16 million leaves 186.1 million entries everywhere else on the network. VicTrack's $42.5 million property income for that same year, spread across that traffic, works out to roughly $0.23 per passenger; using VicTrack's more recent $69.77 million 2024-25 figure against the same 2023-24 traffic base narrows the gap but doesn't close it, to roughly $0.37 per passenger. Either way, Southern Cross earns four to six times as much per person walking through it as the rest of the network manages, combined, per person walking through all of it. Traffic volume alone doesn't explain a gap that size: Flinders Street, the network's single busiest station at 20.345 million entries in 2023-24, barely 1 per cent busier than Southern Cross, has nothing resembling a 25-year commercial concession and no equivalent retail program to show for an almost identical footfall.

### Sunshine: the case study that doesn't need a private consortium to make the point

Southern Cross and the Metro Tunnel show what concentrated commercial rights look like under private management. Sunshine Station shows what their absence looks like under standard public management, and it's a more useful test case than Southern Cross precisely because there's no PPP to point at: Sunshine has always been run the same way as the other ~300 stations covered above.

The numbers either side of Sunshine don't match. The most recent documented retail offering inside its paid ticketed area is a single kiosk, confirmed by a dated photograph from Wongm's Rail Gallery taken in November 2020, with no evidence found of anything added since. Set against that: Sunshine is the site of the $4.1 billion Sunshine Superhub, a combined Commonwealth-Victorian investment and the first stage of the Melbourne Airport Rail project, untangling what's been described as the most complex rail junction outside the CBD, spanning more than six kilometres from West Footscray to Albion, and designed to handle more than 40 trains an hour once finished. Construction began in February 2026, with the first stage due in 2030. Sunshine's own annual patronage, around 1.6 million entries in 2022-23 (the most recent year available, and likely depressed by Metro Tunnel-related construction disruption), is modest next to Flinders Street or Southern Cross today, but the entire premise of a $4.1 billion investment sitting on top of it is that it won't stay modest. Nobody builds a six-kilometre, 40-trains-an-hour interchange to serve a station that's meant to stay a place people pass through on the way to somewhere else.

VicTrack doesn't publish a Sunshine-specific commercial revenue figure, and neither does anyone else, there is no public number to cite, which is itself the point. A station can be the designated future anchor of an entire region's rail network and still not generate enough retail activity for the state to bother breaking out separately in a report. That's the gap this paper's "publicly operated" critique is actually about: not that Sunshine is run badly, or losing money it shouldn't be, but that nobody currently treats the station as the thing a neighbourhood gets built around, rather than a place to board a train. On current public scope, the Superhub project is a transport junction with a concourse extension, not a mixed-use precinct, not a retail or residential development, not a place anyone is planning for people to spend time rather than pass through. A single kiosk under a $4.1 billion junction isn't a funding failure; it's what happens when a station is designed and budgeted as a boarding point, and nothing in the brief asks anyone to design it as anything else.

This is exactly the gap VWHF exists to close, and Sunshine is close to a best-case site for it: confirmed funding, a confirmed construction timeline, and an interchange role that guarantees footfall on a scale most of the network will never see. Five years of construction is also five years during which a station-precinct brief, retail, residential density above the concourse, the kind of joint development VicTrack has already done in miniature at Glen Waverley and Hampton Quarter, could be folded into the build rather than retrofitted once Stage 1 opens onto a paid area that still has just one kiosk in it.

### What "publicly operated" actually delivers today

VicTrack's own program for commercially activating its station land is real, but its track record so far is a short list against the size of the network: Glen Waverley's Town Square and IKON apartments, Jewell, Hampton Quarter, and the Gardiner site freed up by the Level Crossing Removal Project are the named, delivered examples, alongside a handful of proposals still working through planning (a South Yarra consortium scheme, the Metro Tunnel's own over-station developments). That's on the order of a dozen sites against a network of ~309 stations, and the structure of every one of those deals is the same: VicTrack sells the development right or the land outright to a private developer for a one-off payment, rather than keeping the asset and collecting rent on it indefinitely. Independent analysis of the Gardiner sale specifically flags that the state has never disclosed what it actually received for the land. That model, sell once and bank a smaller number, is the opposite of what this paper's VWHF proposes. VWHF isn't "VicTrack, but more of it" — it would require the state to start doing something it doesn't currently do: keep the asset, lease it, and collect the rent forever, the way Civic Nexus and Cross Yarra Partnership already do for themselves at six stations.

It's also worth resisting the temptation to point at Box Hill, Camberwell, or Ringwood's Eastland as proof the rest of the network already has plenty of retail. All three are private shopping centres on land adjacent to, or in Box Hill's case built directly above, the station, not state-owned assets generating rent for VicTrack or the budget. The state's own most direct experiment in owning station retail and renting it out, the Degraves Street subway shops under Flinders Street, sat substantially vacant for decades, widely described as a "neglected white elephant," before a recent restoration. State ownership of a station's commercial space is necessary for the VWHF model to work; on its own, the state's own history shows it has never been sufficient.

### The honest framing

No VAGO report, Infrastructure Victoria study, or government audit states in so many words that "most Victorian stations have little to no commercial activation." That precise claim rests on converging circumstantial evidence rather than one settled source: RMIT University's 2019 walkability research, examining all 230 stations on the network at the time, found that middle-suburban stations such as Bentleigh, Murrumbeena, Mentone, Preston and Reservoir already had the underlying structure to support transit-oriented development but weren't built that way; an independent analysis of the Victorian Government's own open station car park data counted roughly 40,000 dedicated parking spaces network-wide, around 51 hectares, the overwhelming majority still surface lots rather than the multi-deck structures that remain rare enough that the first one anywhere on the network only opened at Box Hill in 1984; and the state's own 2025 push to rezone 60 activity centres around train and tram stops for up to 300,000 new homes by 2051 is itself an admission that most station areas are under-built today, or there'd be nothing for that program to fix. None of that is the same as a station-by-station audit, and this paper should be precise about that gap rather than dress circumstantial evidence up as a settled finding.

What the research does settle is the framing this section opened with. The original concern, that Southern Cross and the Metro Tunnel might be the only real examples, and the rest of the network being publicly run might mean the claw-back savings are smaller than they look, gets the direction of the conclusion backwards. The headcount confirms the claw-back mechanism really is narrow, six stations, not 309. But "publicly run" doesn't mean "already activated, already earning, just needs its commercial revenue redirected from a consortium to VWHF." For the other ~300 stations, there's no consortium to claw anything back from because there's barely any commercial activity happening there in the first place. The $1 billion transport-efficiency figure and the Southern Cross/Metro Tunnel claw-back cases are about a different, narrower mechanism, operating margins on existing franchises and two specific commercial PPPs, than the activation argument the rest of this document makes: turning several hundred largely dormant station precincts into productive, rent-generating land. The two shouldn't be added together, and the second number, still unbuilt and far larger in principle, is the one this paper's VWHF chapters are actually about.

### The PAEC ask

The same transparency problem that runs through this paper's Southern Cross and Metro Tunnel sections applies here, just with worse underlying data. VicTrack publishes a single consolidated "property-related income" line in its annual report, with no station-by-station breakdown of retail tenancies, vacancy rates, or lease revenue, and no public disclosure of what it actually received for one-off sales like the Gardiner site. A Fusion MP's PAEC ask is to require VicTrack and the Department of Transport and Planning to publish a station-by-station commercial leasing register, tenancy counts, vacancy, and revenue, for the same reason this paper asks for Southern Cross's CSP and Cross Yarra Partnership's availability payments to be reconciled in public: right now, nobody outside the department can check either side of this argument against real numbers, only a single statewide total with everything else folded in.

## The North Star: Out-Competing Private Developers, Not Outlawing Them

**The state doesn't need to ban developers — it just needs to build cheaper than them, the way Australia Post and Medicare already do.**

Everything in this document so far is about getting land into productive use and capturing the public's share of the value created by public infrastructure. But there's a piece missing, and it's the one that makes the whole approach cohere: what does the state actually do with the land and capital it's accumulating through VWHF?

The answer isn't to ban private developers, ration land releases to them, or regulate them into submission, all of which are slow, litigable, and politically toxic. The answer is for the state to become a competitor that private developers can't out-compete on the things that actually matter to buyers and renters: price.

Victorians already live with two institutions that work exactly this way, and nobody calls either of them socialism. Australia Post doesn't ban DHL, FedEx or Toll from operating — it just runs a parcel and letter service with no profit margin requirement, no dividend obligation to shareholders chasing quarterly returns, and no marketing arms race, and that's enough to set a floor under price and service that every private courier has to match or differentiate away from. Medicare doesn't ban private health insurance — it guarantees a baseline of care free at the point of use, which forces private insurers to actually compete on something, speed, choice, amenity, rather than "pay us or get nothing."

VWHF, building at every transit-oriented site it controls, does the same thing to the housing and commercial development market. It builds at cost: no developer margin, no land-banked unsold stock managed to protect price, no marketing spend beyond what's needed to lease or sell, and it prices the result slightly below prevailing market rate for comparable stock. It doesn't need to dominate the market to work. It needs a presence in enough transit precincts that every private developer bidding against it knows their product has to be better, cheaper, or faster to deliver than the state's — or it doesn't sell.

This is what ties the rest of the paper together. The inclusionary zoning rule, the cumulative car park tax, and the Transit Capital Fund all operate on the supply side: they get land into productive use and extract the public's share of the value public infrastructure creates. The public developer operates on the demand side, as permanent competitive pressure that makes land-banking, staged drip-feed releases, and margin-protection a losing long-term strategy. A land-banker doesn't just face an escalating tax bill for sitting still — they face a state-backed competitor that will build on the lot next door, at cost, while they wait.

Most private developers will keep operating, the same way most parcels still move through DHL and most people still hold private health cover. The point isn't to eliminate them. It's that the state shouldn't have to subsidise, beg, or regulate an industry that's chronically underbuilding a public good, when it has the balance sheet, the land, and the financing model (via VWHF's Social Infrastructure Bonds) to simply build the floor itself.

### The honest framing on crowding out

The obvious objection to a state developer pricing at cost is that it doesn't just discipline the private market, it displaces it — and that objection has real precedent, not just theory. Separate strands of US housing research back this up from two different angles: the Faircloth Amendment (1998), which bans federal funding from expanding the public housing stock beyond 1999 levels, is a direct legislative response to the argument that government-built housing crowds out private investment rather than adding to total supply, while independent empirical work on public construction's effect on private housing starts has produced displacement estimates in the broad vicinity of one private-market unit lost for every two to three publicly-built units — the exact ratio varies by study and market, but the direction of the effect doesn't. Even Singapore's HDB, the most successful state-developer model anywhere, doesn't escape the dynamic: at 82% of the housing stock, HDB doesn't operate alongside the private market so much as set its terms, and shifts in HDB eligibility or resale rules ripple directly into private prices.

VWHF should expect the same thing to happen, and the honest answer is that this isn't a flaw in the plan, it's the plan working as intended. Compressing the margin available to private developers on comparable stock is what "the state sets the floor" means in practice — it's the same mechanism by which Australia Post caps what couriers can charge and Medicare puts a floor under what insurers have to offer, and nobody describes those as accidental side effects. The difference between VWHF and the US public-housing experience that produced the Faircloth Amendment is scale and footprint: Faircloth was a reaction to large, concentrated public housing estates competing for the same dollars as everything else in the federal budget, not a targeted presence in specific transit precincts where land-banking and informal price-setting are the actual problem. If some marginal private projects in those specific precincts don't proceed because an at-cost public competitor makes them unviable at the margin a developer wanted, that is the surcharge and the public developer doing the same job from two directions — which is also the reason VWHF's footprint should be concentrated in the precincts where the problem is worst, rather than spread thin across the state, so the crowding-out lands where it's doing the most good and the private sector retains the rest of the market it currently underuses anyway.

One specific challenge raised in debate under the "capital flight" label: the claim that if VWHF is the developer of record — rather than a regulator of private developers — private capital will exit transit-precinct markets entirely, and the net supply gain will be smaller than the gross public-build figure suggests. This is directionally real, as the crowding-out research above confirms. But even the highest plausible displacement ratio — one private unit lost for every two to three public units built — still produces a net gain of one to two units over what would otherwise be built in those precincts, because the current public baseline is zero. Zero new public units are being built in the relevant precincts under the status quo. Treating a net positive as a downside because it is smaller than the gross positive requires accepting that zero is the number worth defending, which is not a position this paper endorses.

## **Active Wealth Creation & Land Productivity**

**Make land-banking near stations a guaranteed loss — every year a site sits idle, the cost of sitting on it climbs.**

The state shouldn't just act as a passive planner. To structurally out-earn our liabilities, the Victorian Wealth & Housing Fund VWHF will deploy capital into revenue-generating commercial assets and enforce maximum productivity on land next to public transport hubs.

The VWHF will operate as a primary commercial developer at major transport interchanges. While 10% of residential builds above these hubs are kept for public

housing, the remaining 90% of residential space and 100% of the retail footprint (such as integrated station shopping precincts) will be leased directly to the market. This creates a permanent, high-yield property portfolio for the state, with market rents flowing straight into the treasury to retire general debt.

Commercial surface car parks within 1 km of metropolitan train stations are a total failure of land use, but they're only the most visible example of a wider problem: a 1 km radius around a train station is precisely the footprint of a walkable mixed-use neighbourhood, and Victoria is full of stations surrounded by single-use commercial land — surface car parks, single-storey big-box retail, showrooms, self-storage, light-industrial sheds — sitting on some of the best-located land in the state and doing almost nothing with it. Every year one of these sites stays single-use is a year of land-banking: holding an underused site near infrastructure the public just paid to build, and waiting for its value to keep rising before doing anything with it.

**This applies to commercial land only, and explicitly excludes a person's principal place of residence.** Nothing in this mechanism touches a family home, a duplex someone lives in, or a small residential block — even if it's within 1 km of a station and even if it's technically "underdeveloped" relative to what zoning allows. The entire point of the inclusionary zoning and density-bonus framework above is to add housing without forcing anyone out of their existing home, and a land-banking surcharge that hit homeowners would be the exact opposite of that. This is a tax on commercially held land sitting idle for profit, not a backdoor mechanism to pressure residents off their property.

A flat land tax surcharge doesn't fix the commercial land-banking problem. A flat annual cost is just a number an owner can price into how long they're prepared to wait — if the land is appreciating faster than the surcharge, waiting still wins, every year, forever.

So make it cumulative, not flat. Every financial year a commercial site within 1 km of a metropolitan train station remains in a single-use, low-intensity holding category — surface car park, vacant lot, single-storey retail or showroom on a site zoned for towers, light-industrial use on land zoned mixed-use — the surcharge climbs by a further increment on top of the previous year's rate: for example, 2% of unimproved land value in year one, rising by 2 percentage points every subsequent year the site stays in that category. By year five the holding cost is 10% of land value annually; by year ten, 20%. For sites the state wants to send an unmistakable signal about — say, anything within 400 metres of a Suburban Rail Loop station — the increment itself can compound rather than simply add, putting genuinely indefinite land-banking on an exponential cost curve.

This inverts the landowner's calculus completely. Under no surcharge, or a flat one, time is free: the land appreciates while the carrying cost stays flat or at zero, so holding is always at least as good as building. Under a cumulative surcharge, time becomes the enemy — every year of inaction makes the next year more expensive, regardless of what the land itself is doing. Calibrated properly, the surcharge overtakes any plausible annual capital gain well within a single parliamentary term. Past that point, holding isn't just less attractive than building — it's a guaranteed loss-making position, and it keeps getting worse the longer it's ignored.

The surcharge resets to 0% only if the site is redeveloped to a mixed-use or residential outcome consistent with its planning-permitted density (for car parks specifically, a consolidated multi-storey structure that shrinks the surface footprint by at least 75% also qualifies), or sold — to a private developer who will build, or to the state itself via VWHF, at the discount a structurally loss-making holding now commands. There is no fourth option. Build, sell to someone who will build, or sell to the state. Revenue from owners who keep paying anyway flows into the Transit Capital Fund, but the mechanism isn't designed around collecting it — it's designed to make collecting it rare, because the entire point is that nobody should find it rational to keep paying for long.

### Statewide: hide the parking or pay for it

Separately from the station-radius mechanism above, every commercial surface car park in Victoria, regardless of distance from a train station, faces a flat annual surcharge unless the operator either consolidates parking into a structured (multi-storey or basement) facility or redevelops the site. A surface car park is the least productive possible use of land anywhere, not just near stations — it's a statement that the land underneath is worth less than the convenience of not building anything on it. The choice is simple: build up or down and put the land back to productive use, or pay an ongoing surcharge that reflects the opportunity cost of an entire allotment dedicated to storing empty cars at ground level. This doesn't need the cumulative escalator that the station-radius version does — a flat-but-meaningful statewide surcharge is enough to make "just put a structure around the parking" the obviously cheaper option almost everywhere, while the cumulative version is reserved for the highest-value, transit-adjacent land where the state wants land-banking to become untenable on a timeline, not just unprofitable.

### Transition mechanics: a worked example — Woodgrove, Melton

**Everything above describes the destination and the financial incentive to get there. It doesn't describe the journey, and for a centre that's trading every day with thousands of customers walking through it, the journey is the part that actually has to work.** Woodgrove already came up earlier in this paper as Melton's de facto town centre and the public-transport blind spot where it's currently easier to get there from Caroline Springs than from inside Melton itself (see "Melbourne's West, North and South East" below). It's also a useful stress test for the parking mechanism above, because it's exactly the kind of site that mechanism targets and exactly the kind of site critics are right to ask "okay, but how": somewhere in the order of 50,000-60,000 m² of single-storey retail behind roughly 2,400 surface car spaces, privately owned (Assembly Funds Management and PGIM Real Estate, not the state), about 900 metres, an easy 12-minute walk almost nobody currently makes, from Melton railway station. Woodgrove doesn't clear the 1 km station-radius trigger by much, which is exactly why the _statewide_ flat surcharge described above, the one that applies to every commercial surface car park regardless of distance from a train station, is the lever that reaches it. VWHF isn't the builder here; the existing private owner is, and the surcharge is what gives that owner a structural reason to stop sitting on 2,400 free surface spaces.

![Woodgrove Shopping Centre today: a single-storey big-box format behind a dominant surface car park, around 900 metres from Melton railway station](images/woodgrove-today.jpg)
_Woodgrove today — the baseline this section starts from._

**Move the buses before you move the cars, not after.** The standard failure mode of "shrink the car park" policy is treating parking removal and transit investment as one simultaneous event, because that's how it looks in a render. Pull spaces before a credible alternative exists and the result isn't fewer car trips, it's the same number of trips fighting over fewer spaces, and the next decade's cautionary tale for why road diets don't work. The sequencing has to run the other way, and at Woodgrove it already has a real anchor to run from: Infrastructure Victoria's August 2025 strategy, _Fast, frequent, fair: how buses can better connect Melbourne_, already names a Melton-to-Broadmeadows corridor as one of six statewide bus rapid transit priorities for the early 2030s, targeting the dedicated-lane, 5-10 minute frequency that makes a bus function like a service rather than a timetable to plan a day around. This paper's addition to that roadmap is narrow: treat that corridor's Melton terminus as a Transit Development District trigger in its own right, on the same statutory footing as a train station, not as an amenity that arrives only after a developer has already broken ground. A dedicated lane terminating at Woodgrove's own door, not at a stop across the car park, is what has to exist before a single space comes out, because it's what makes removing spaces later survivable instead of punitive.

**Route 454 is the nearer-term version of this same anchor, and it's worth being honest about exactly how near-term.** Separately from the early-2030s BRT corridor above, the state's April 2026 "Bus Bonanza" package, near $100 million folded into the 2026-27 Budget, already funds a doubling of Route 454's frequency and a new direct connection from Melton and Cobblebank stations to Woodgrove's own door, money confirmed now rather than a decade-out roadmap item. That makes it the better candidate for an immediate Transit Development District trigger at Woodgrove, but only from the date it's actually running, not the date it was announced. The same package's funding for Route 140, promised in the prior year's budget to finally connect the isolated Mount Atkinson estate to the rail network, still had no confirmed start date as of the April 2026 announcement and didn't carry its first passenger (launched as the 140a service) until 28 June 2026, well over a year after it was first funded; transport advocates labelled the wider package a "drip-feed" for exactly this reason. Treat Route 454's enhanced service, once it's verifiably running at the promised frequency to Woodgrove's door, as the trigger date, and Woodgrove gets two real anchors instead of one: a modest but already-funded near-term frequency upgrade, and the fuller BRT spine behind it in the early 2030s.

![Concept render of a dedicated bus rapid transit lane running through the redeveloped Woodgrove precinct, with priority lane markings and solar canopies overhead](images/woodgrove-brt-concept.png)
_Illustrative concept render (AI-generated) of what "direct to door" looks like once the BRT spine is in. Not an official design or proposal._

**Consolidation comes before subtraction.** Once the bus spine is running, the flat surcharge gives the owner a live financial reason to act, and the first new structure built is parking, not towers: a multi-storey deck absorbing a meaningful share of the existing surface spaces on a fraction of their footprint, freeing the rest of the lot for the first stage of mixed-use frontage facing the new bus lane. Woodgrove has already proven that staged construction around a trading centre is something this specific site can do without closing — it's done it at least four times since the late 1990s (a 1998 Kmart and specialty-shop expansion, a 2003 cinema and office addition, a 2006 medical centre and Bunnings, and a $150 million 2013 redevelopment that more than doubled the centre's footprint). Nothing about the construction logistics changes when the direction reverses; only the destination does. And because the bus spine already exists by this point, every following stage of construction converts another slice of surface lot to built form while the structure absorbing the displaced cars gets to assume something less than one-for-one replacement, because a 5-10 minute bus direct to the door has already pulled a meaningful share of routine trips, the weekly grocery run, the school-holiday trip to Kmart, away from needing a car at all. That's the same "let parking shrink with demand" principle already built into transit-oriented planning codes; Woodgrove just demonstrates it on an existing site instead of mandating it on a hypothetical one.

![Aerial concept render of the redeveloped Woodgrove precinct: mid-rise mixed-use buildings with solar canopy roofs flanking a central transit and pedestrian spine, with a single consolidated parking structure rather than a surface lot](images/woodgrove-mature-precinct-concept.png)
_Illustrative concept render (AI-generated) of the mature end state — built up, bus-fed, and with parking consolidated to a fraction of today's footprint. Not an official design or proposal._

**That end state is what "hide the parking or pay for it" actually cashes out to once it's walked through a real site instead of stated as a rule**: a single consolidated structure, built-up frontage either side of a dedicated transit spine, and a car park that's shrunk because fewer people need it, not because they've been told to manage without one.

### The honest framing on timeline

Every stage above is years long, not months, and the first one is the longest: Infrastructure Victoria's own roadmap puts the Melton-to-Broadmeadows corridor in the early 2030s, which means the bus has to land before anything else in this section starts, and that alone is most of a decade out from when this paper is being written. The renders accompanying this section show the destination, not a forecast of what Woodgrove looks like next term or the term after, and reading them as a near-term commitment would be reading them wrong. What they're there to make legible is the _order_: bus first, consolidation second, density third, and parking shrinkage last, earned by falling demand rather than imposed against it. The mechanism that makes the transition financially inevitable is the surcharge described above; how long it takes to actually bite is exactly as long as it takes a private owner sitting on appreciating, no-longer-free land to decide that paying an escalating cost to keep doing nothing is worse than building.

### Why the seed debt is a non-issue

Every one of these redevelopment sites is currently carried on the state's books as "surface car park" — among the lowest-value classifications a piece of land near a train station can have. The moment VWHF rezones one of these sites for mixed-income housing or a commercial precinct, even before a single shovel goes into the ground, the asset's valuation on the state's balance sheet re-rates to reflect its highest and best use, which for a site within 1 km of a transit hub is typically multiples of its car-park valuation.

That means the debt VWHF issues to fund the initial construction at a given site is, from day one, sitting against an asset that has already appreciated by more than the amount borrowed, independent of and well before the rental income described above ever starts flowing. The net asset position improves immediately; net debt, in the narrow headline sense the Coalition likes to quote, barely moves, because it's offset by a corresponding jump in the value of state-owned land. This is the same loan-to-value logic that underpins every real estate developer's funding decision, and it is exactly the kind of "borrowing to build an asset" that this paper argues is structurally different from borrowing to cover an operating shortfall.

A common objection at this point is the household budget analogy: "even treating the state like a household, debt is still debt." The analogy is right on its own terms and wrong in its conclusion. A household doesn't borrow to fund a holiday and call it an investment. But it does take out a mortgage — debt — to buy a property, specifically because the moment a slab goes down on a vacant block, the property's value rises above what was borrowed and equity is created before the first tenant walks through the door. That is "borrowing to build an asset" in its most familiar, everyday form. What this paper proposes is using that same mechanism at state scale, on land the state already controls, against the very property developers who currently benefit from publicly funded infrastructure without contributing to it. We're using the property developers' playbook against the property developers, for the state's benefit. The phrase "debt is debt" is only true if you ignore what the debt bought.

### The honest framing on enforcement

Victoria already has a live case study for how this kind of surcharge plays out, and it isn't all good news. The Vacant Residential Land Tax (VRLT), expanded statewide from January 2025, has drawn sustained criticism from the Property Council of Australia for penalising owners over delays they don't fully control — financing falling through, labour shortages, planning appeals — and treating every stalled site as deliberate land-banking when some genuinely aren't. Tax advisers have also documented owners routing around VRLT entirely through ambiguous exemption categories and a non-disclosure penalty that's rarely enforced. A cumulative surcharge that escalates every year an owner is deemed to be sitting on their hands will face the same complaint, louder, because the stakes per year are higher.

There's a second, sharper risk drawn from vacant-land-tax experience more broadly: where holding "vacant land" is taxed more lightly than holding an underused building, owners sometimes respond by demolishing what's there rather than redeveloping — Detroit spent over $34 million between 2014 and 2019 demolishing properties abandoned for exactly this reason. A surcharge that's easier to escape by knocking a building down than by building something new is a surcharge that makes blight worse, not better.

Both objections need a direct answer, not a hand-wave. On the first: the surcharge should pause, not reset, for a site with an active, lodged development application or building permit — an owner genuinely working through the planning system isn't land-banking, and the clock stops while they wait on a council or referral authority, but starts again the moment the application lapses or is withdrawn. That's a narrower carve-out than VRLT's because it requires the owner to be doing something, not merely intending to. On the second: because the surcharge is keyed to the _land use category_ — surface car park, vacant lot, single-storey retail on a tower site — rather than to whether a structure currently exists, demolishing a building doesn't lower the rate or reset the clock. A cleared lot in a single-use commercial category is still a single-use commercial holding, and the surcharge keeps climbing exactly as it would have. The Detroit dynamic exists because vacant land was the _cheaper_ category to be in; this design makes sure it never is. If anything, an owner who demolishes a building as a stalling tactic has made their position strictly worse — they've spent money removing the one thing (an operating tenant, a heritage constraint, an active lease) that might otherwise have supported a delay exemption.

### A third risk: "permit-banking."

Pausing the surcharge during pre-development and permitting is the right design, it protects genuine builders from being taxed into distress mid-project, but it also creates an exploit. A speculative owner with no intention of building can lodge a token, minimal, or deliberately over-complicated application, or run it into a protracted VCAT appeal, and sit in "active pre-development" indefinitely while the clock stays paused, achieving exactly the land-banking outcome the surcharge exists to prevent, just with extra paperwork. The fix has to be statutory, not discretionary: the legislation establishing the surcharge needs a tight, time-limited definition of "active pre-development", a hard cap on how long an application or appeal can hold the pause before the clock resumes regardless of its status, and a requirement that the application represent a genuine, planning-compliant proposal at something close to the site's permitted density, not a token lodgement designed only to stop the clock. None of this is a reason not to build the mechanism; it's a drafting requirement for the mechanism, and one the VWHF legislation needs to get right at the same time as the surcharge itself, not as a patch afterwards. It's also where the construction-phase exemption above needs to stay tightly coupled to the same statutory test: genuine builders get the pause, genuine builders only.

On transition: a 12-month pause from the date the legislation takes effect is the right design — a defined runway with no ambiguity about when the clock starts. Beyond that window, the surcharge applies in full, with extremely limited exceptions as described above. A longer grace period defeats the mechanism by extending the period during which holding remains costless; the 12-month window is there to ensure site owners had genuine notice and a reasonable opportunity to act. The design of what constitutes a valid exception beyond that window is a drafting question for the enabling legislation; the length of the transition period is not.

## The Crime Connection: Why This Is Also a Law and Order Policy

**Cost of living and housing insecurity are Victoria's number one polling issue — and the upstream cause of the crime debate too.**

Every poll for the last two years has put crime, specifically aggravated burglary, car theft, and youth offending, at or near the top of Victorian voters' concerns. The major parties' response is uniform: more police, tougher bail laws, more remand. None of it touches the upstream cause.

Crime statistics and criminology research consistently link property crime and youth offending rates to two structural factors: housing insecurity (overcrowding, frequent forced moves, homelessness and near-homelessness) and acute cost-of-living pressure on low-income households. When a household is one rent increase away from losing its home, or skipping meals to make a car payment, the population most exposed to that stress, disproportionately young people, is also the population most likely to end up in the offending statistics, whether as victims, witnesses to family stress, or eventually as offenders themselves.

This is the connection the major parties can't make, because making it means admitting their housing and cost-of-living settings are part of the crime problem. Fusion can make it, because the rest of this document is a plan to fix exactly those settings: 30,000 new mixed-income, rent-stabilised homes taken out of the private rental market's pressure cooker; a structural floor under the cost of housing near transit, where most low-income renters need to live to access jobs; and, with the dividend mechanism below, a direct, recurring cash buffer for households living closest to the edge.

None of this replaces policing. But "we are addressing the economic conditions that drive the crime wave" is a sentence the major parties cannot say with a straight face, because they'd have to admit their own budgets created those conditions. Fusion can say it, and back it with the numbers in this paper.

## Closing the Loop: The Westfield Clip and the Victorian Dividend

**Once VWHF owns these precincts, every Victorian gets a direct cash dividend — modelled on Alaska's Permanent Fund, but built so it can't be raided.**

Everything above gets the state to asset recovery: VWHF owns productive land, Southern Cross's commercial revenue comes back in-house, and the Transit Capital Fund is collecting land value uplift. But asset recovery on its own is just a better-run government. The final step, the one that makes this pitch land with people who don't care about balance sheets, is what happens to the money once it's flowing.

### The Westfield clip

Scentre Group, which runs Westfield in Australia, doesn't just charge retail tenants a flat rent. It charges a base rent plus a percentage of the tenant's turnover above an agreed threshold, so the landlord's income rises automatically as the centre gets busier, without ever renegotiating the lease. There is no reason VWHF-owned retail precincts, the station shopping strips, the Southern Cross retail tenancies once Civic Nexus's contract lapses, the ground-floor retail in every transit-oriented development VWHF builds, should be leased any differently. A turnover-rent clip on VWHF's commercial tenancies means the fund's income grows in line with the actual economic activity happening on its land, not just with fixed indexation.

### Why a clip, not just rent: the flywheel this creates

The reason to choose this model specifically, rather than just charging the highest base rent the market will bear, is the incentive it creates for VWHF, and by extension the state, as landlord. A landlord on fixed rent alone is indifferent to whether its tenants thrive or merely survive; its income is the same either way, and its only lever for more revenue is raising rent, which is the part of this model that reads as predatory if it's the whole story. A landlord with a turnover clip has a direct financial stake in its tenants doing more business: more foot traffic, longer dwell times, a tenant mix that draws people in and keeps them spending. That gives VWHF a built-in reason to actively manage its precincts, programming public space, curating tenancy mix, investing in the amenity around the stations it owns, rather than just collecting cheques, because every dollar of extra turnover it helps generate is a dollar that flows back as clip revenue. That's the flywheel: VWHF's own commercial incentive is to increase the turnover of the businesses it hosts, which grows the businesses, which grows the precinct, which grows VWHF's income, which funds more of the development and amenity that grows turnover further.

This is also the answer to the "isn't this just the state taking a second bite, base rent _and_ a cut of turnover" objection. The honest framing is that the clip isn't an extra charge layered on top of an otherwise-market rent for the same reason a private landlord does it: it's how the landlord's incentives get aligned with the tenant's success instead of sitting apart from it. In practice this means VWHF, like Scentre Group, would set the base rent lower than a pure fixed-rent landlord would, with the clip making up the difference only when a tenant is actually doing well, which is the opposite of predatory: a struggling tenant pays less than they would under a flat-rent model, and a thriving one pays more, but only because they're thriving. Getting that balance, base rent low enough to attract and retain tenants, clip calibrated so it doesn't tax success so heavily that it drives tenants (or the spending that would otherwise happen on VWHF's land) elsewhere, is a real design question VWHF's commercial arm would need to get right, and getting it wrong in either direction has a real cost: too high a clip risks the kind of capital flight this section is trying to avoid, while too low a base rent undermines the income case for the fund altogether. But that's a calibration problem for VWHF's commercial managers to solve with the same lease-negotiation tools Scentre Group already uses, not a flaw in the underlying logic of why the model is the right one to start from.

### The Victorian Dividend

Once VWHF's combined income streams, rental income, the turnover clip, the captured land value uplift via the Transit Capital Fund, and the Southern Cross-style recovered commercial revenue, exceed the fund's debt service and reinvestment commitments, the surplus doesn't just disappear into consolidated revenue. A statutory share of it, modelled on the Alaska Permanent Fund's annual dividend paid to Alaskan residents out of the state's oil revenue since 1982, is paid out annually, per person, as a direct cash dividend to Victorian residents.

### Starting narrow, scaling wide

A statewide per-person dividend funded from a portfolio that's still mostly under construction would be trivial — a few dollars a year, easy for critics to dismiss as a gimmick in its own right. So don't start there. In its first phase, the dividend (or an equivalent rent rebate) is paid to tenants living in VWHF housing and to businesses leased into VWHF commercial precincts — the people whose rent is the surplus, getting a direct share of it back. That's a small population, but it makes the per-person amount meaningful from day one, and it's the most honest possible proof of concept: the state, as landlord, is visibly sharing the upside with the people paying it rent, rather than just pocketing the difference between "at cost" and "market." As VWHF's footprint expands, both the eligible population and the surplus per person grow together, and the dividend widens outward, first to residents of the suburbs and transit precincts where VWHF is the dominant landlord, and eventually statewide once the portfolio is large enough that a universal payment is more than a rounding error. The end state is still a Victorian Dividend for every resident; the path there starts with the people VWHF already has a direct relationship with, which is also the cohort best placed to notice, and talk about, the difference it makes.

This is the step the document has been missing. Without it, the pitch is "trust the state to manage assets better," which is a hard sell to anyone who's watched a decade of Big Build cost blowouts. With it, the pitch becomes concrete and personal: the state owns the car park outside your station, the retail strip you walk through every day, and the shopping centre near the rail line, and because it owns them, you get a cheque. That's the sentence that travels on social media. It converts an abstract argument about balance sheets into the only thing that actually changes minds: someone's own bank account.

### What a mature VWHF household could realistically see

Start from the bottom up, with what this paper has already established, rather than working backwards from a target figure, and start by being honest about scale: **30,000 homes is the first five-year tranche, not VWHF's ceiling.** VWHF's mandate, as set out above, is to declare every new Suburban Rail Loop, Metro Tunnel and regional rail station corridor a TDD and build Rail+Property-style mixed-use precincts on each of them. Run that mandate for two to three decades, not five years, and the realistic footprint isn't 30,000 dwellings, it's a multiple of that, plausibly somewhere in the **100,000-180,000 dwelling** range across dozens of station precincts, each with its own retail, office and commercial component.

That changes the asset-base estimate substantially. Stack 100,000-180,000 completed dwellings and their commercial precincts on top of VicTrack's existing landholdings (already the fourth-largest landowner in Victoria), the Port of Melbourne lease value, and Homes Victoria's existing ~65,000-dwelling portfolio, and a mature VWHF is plausibly carrying an asset base in the order of **$100-250 billion**, not $30-50 billion. There's a flywheel effect built into the top end of that range, too: if VWHF precincts genuinely become some of the most desirable places in the state to live, work and set up shop, the "Westfield clip" turnover-rent mechanism and the precincts' own rental values both run hotter than an average-market assumption would suggest, which is exactly the kind of compounding this paper's "why a clip, not just rent" section argues VWHF is structurally set up to capture.

A genuine net surplus _after_ bond service and reinvestment of something like 2-3% of asset value a year, on a $100-250 billion base, is **$2.0-7.5 billion a year** in distributable surplus, not $600 million-$1.5 billion. The tenant-first cohort scales with the housing stock too: 100,000-180,000 households, call it roughly **150,000 households, or around 345,000 people** at an average household size of 2.3, not the ~70,000-person figure a 30,000-dwelling footprint implies.

Paid to that cohort, $2.0-7.5 billion a year works out to $5,800-21,700 per person per year, or roughly **$112-418 a week per person**, **$258-962/week for a 2-person household**.

That cash dividend is only one part of what living in a VWHF precinct is worth, though. The rest of this paper has already quantified two more pieces:

- **Not needing a car**: the "cost-of-living relief" section earlier in this paper cites the AAA's Transport Affordability Index putting annual car costs, fuel, registration, insurance and maintenance, at $8,000-$12,000 a year even before loan repayments. A household in a genuinely walkable, transit-oriented VWHF precinct that drops a car entirely saves **$150-230/week**, every week, indefinitely.
- **Below-market rent**: VWHF prices its housing "slightly below prevailing market rate" by design (see "The North Star" section above). On a typical Melbourne rent, even a modest discount is worth roughly **$55-80/week**.

Add those three together and a mature VWHF household lands in the **$463-1,272/week** range. The distinction that matters for how this gets communicated: the dividend is the only piece that's a literal payment, the other two are costs that don't get incurred in the first place because of how the precinct is built. That's not a weaker claim, a dollar not spent on a car is worth exactly as much as a dollar received, but it does mean this figure describes what it's like to live somewhere VWHF has built, not the size of a single cheque from VWHF. Either way, it's a real number, it falls out of this paper's own VWHF modelling at a scale consistent with VWHF's actual mandate, and it didn't need a pre-set target to get there. Notably, **$800/week now sits inside this range on its own**, at the upper-middle rather than requiring the most optimistic possible assumptions, which is a meaningfully different starting point for the next section than the $30-50 billion estimate produced.

### $800/week as a north star: a precursor to a UBI, not a substitute for one

**$800 a week per person, roughly $41,600 a year, wasn't where the VWHF numbers above started.** It's a figure that's been floated separately, as a north star for what a _Universal Basic Income_ could look like, and it's worth being precise about where it now sits relative to the corrected VWHF numbers above, and what role it can still usefully play beyond VWHF.

**As a per-person figure for the tenant-first cohort, $800/week is now a stretch rather than impossible.** $41,600/year × ~345,000 people is about $14.4 billion a year, which at a 3% payout implies an asset base of roughly $480 billion, around two to five times the $100-250 billion mature-VWHF estimate above. That's no longer "off by orders of magnitude", it's "VWHF would need to roughly double or triple even its expanded, multi-decade, flywheel-assisted footprint." Genuinely possible over a long enough horizon if VWHF's market-investment arm (the GIC-style diversification discussed earlier) compounds for long enough, but it's the ceiling of an optimistic scenario, not the realistic mid-point, which is why the household range above, $463-1,272/week, is the number this paper stands behind, with $800/week sitting inside it rather than at its edge.

**As a statewide, per-person figure, $800/week remains a different conversation, though the gap has narrowed.** Victoria's population is roughly 7 million. $2.0-7.5 billion a year in VWHF surplus, spread across all 7 million Victorians rather than just the tenant-first cohort, works out to $286-1,071 per person per year, or roughly **$5.50-20.60 a week**, a real and meaningful improvement on the "a few dollars a year" this paper's earlier, smaller VWHF estimate implied, but still a long way short of $41,600/year. For VWHF's surplus to sustainably throw off $291 billion a year statewide at a 3% payout rate, the fund's asset base would need to be on the order of **$9.7 trillion**, somewhere between 40 and 100 times the $100-250 billion mature-VWHF estimate, and still comfortably larger than the entire pool of Australian superannuation savings across every fund in the country combined. The gap has gone from "three orders of magnitude" to "nearly two", which matters, but it's still not a gap VWHF closes on its own for every Victorian, only for the people living and working in the precincts it actually builds.

**At a federal level, the picture is genuinely different.** A UBI, in the form usually debated federally, is an unconditional cash payment to every adult, funded out of general tax revenue, typically alongside a rationalisation of existing welfare payments (JobSeeker, Family Tax Benefit, and so on) and changes to income tax thresholds and offsets to pay for it. That's a tax-and-transfer redesign operating on a base, total Commonwealth tax revenue and welfare spending, that's roughly an order of magnitude larger than anything a single state's asset portfolio can touch. For a statewide-equivalent $800/week, a federal welfare-and-tax-reform exercise is still the far more plausible route than VWHF's own balance sheet, even on the corrected, much larger VWHF numbers above. This paper doesn't attempt that federal costing, it's a different debate, at a different level of government, but it's the reason $800/week statewide is the right order of magnitude for _that_ conversation, while $800/week to VWHF's own residents is now something this paper's own numbers can plausibly support.

**What the Victorian Dividend can do, that's genuinely within a state's reach, is prove the concept at a scale Victoria actually controls**: that a government can own productive assets, run a surplus after debt service, and pay a share of it directly and recurrently to residents, with the payment structurally insulated from the operating budget (the "honest framing on sustainability" below is the mechanism that makes that insulation real). That's the part of a UBI that's hardest to win politically, not the amount, but the _principle_ that the state hands money back directly rather than only ever delivering value through services. A state-level dividend that starts small, is real, and visibly grows is a working demonstration of that principle long before any federal UBI debate produces legislation, and on the corrected numbers above, "visibly grows" can plausibly mean growing into the $800/week range for VWHF's own residents within this paper's own mandate, not just as a borrowed federal aspiration.

### Stacking the two: what $1,600/week+ would actually mean

The honest relationship between the Victorian Dividend and a future federal UBI is sequencing and scale, not competition. If a federal UBI or negative-income-tax reform ever does land at something in the order of $800/week per household, it doesn't replace the Victorian Dividend, it sits on top of it, the same way Alaska's PFD sits on top of federal welfare payments for Alaskans rather than substituting for them.

Stack the two and the combined number for a mature VWHF household becomes genuinely transformative: the **$463-1,272/week** in cash dividend plus avoided car and rent costs that VWHF alone could plausibly deliver to its own residents, _plus_ a federal UBI in the order of $800/week, takes a household to somewhere around **$1,600-2,000/week or more** in combined cash and avoided costs. That's not "comfortable", that's a figure that, for a household currently living payday-to-payday, changes what's possible: it's the difference between rent stress and a deposit being saveable, between "can't afford the car repair" and not needing the car at all, between cost-of-living being the dominant fact of a household's life and it not being.

The reason to put this number in the paper at all, while being honest that it depends on a federal reform this paper doesn't control or cost, is that it's the clearest illustration of why the sequencing argument above matters. Victoria doesn't have to wait for a federal UBI to start delivering the $535-1,130/week piece, and a federal UBI, if it ever arrives, doesn't make VWHF redundant, it makes VWHF households the first in the country to feel the _combined_ effect of both. Two governments each doing the thing only they can do, stacked on top of each other, is how $800/week stops being a single ambitious number from either level of government and becomes $1,600/week+ from both.

### The honest framing on sustainability

The Alaska comparison is worth being honest about, because the Permanent Fund Dividend's recent history is as much a cautionary tale as an inspiration. Declining oil revenue opened a structural gap between what Alaska earns and what it spends; the legislature capped annual fund draws at 5% of value (the "POMV" rule) in 2018, and has repeatedly exceeded that cap anyway. In the 2026 budget cycle the dividend was provisionally set to zero amid that fight — proof that a dividend drawn from the same pool as general spending is the first thing cut when the budget is under pressure, regardless of what was promised when the scheme launched.

The design difference that matters here: Alaska's PFD is paid from the fund's overall earnings, which puts it in direct competition with the government's general spending needs for the same dollars — that's exactly the setup that invites a raid when oil revenue falls short. The Victorian Dividend is structured differently on purpose: it's paid only from VWHF's surplus _after_ debt service and reinvestment, money that doesn't exist in the general government accounts at all unless VWHF's portfolio is already throwing off more cash than it needs to service its bonds and keep building. That makes it self-limiting in the right direction — in a lean year the dividend is small or zero because there genuinely isn't a surplus, not because a Treasurer redirected money earmarked for households to plug an unrelated hole, and there's no general-revenue line for a future government to raid in the first place.

The trade-off is honesty about timing: this isn't a mechanism that pays out meaningfully statewide in year one, and selling it as an immediate cheque-in-the-mail for every Victorian would be overpromising. The tenant-first phasing above is the answer to that: rather than wait years for a universal payment large enough to matter, or launch a universal payment too small to matter, the dividend is real and meaningful from the start for a smaller group, and grows into the universal version as VWHF does. The realistic pitch is closer to a future fund than to Alaska's PFD circa 1982: a dividend that starts targeted, grows as the portfolio matures, and is insulated from the operating budget by how it's constructed — not a substitute for near-term cost-of-living relief delivered through other channels, but a standing commitment that compounds the longer VWHF is left to do its job.

A structural risk worth naming directly: a Victorian Dividend paid initially to a narrow cohort — VWHF tenants and precinct businesses — lacks the broad political constituency that protects Alaska's PFD from year-to-year budget pressure. A payment to 70,000–345,000 people is much easier for a future Treasurer to quietly reclassify as consolidated revenue, citing a budget shortfall, than a payment to every Victorian would be. The mitigant is statutory: the legislation establishing the Victorian Dividend needs to ring-fence VWHF's distributable surplus from the Consolidated Fund with the same legal force as the ring-fence on VWHF's own capital. That doesn't make it raid-proof — any legislature can pass amending legislation — but "requires new primary legislation to redirect" is a meaningfully higher bar than "Treasurer varies a line item in a budget bill." The narrow starting cohort is a genuine political vulnerability on long-run sustainability; the answer is to get the statutory protection right from day one, not to delay establishing the dividend until the cohort is broad enough to be self-defending politically. Once a precedent is set that VWHF's surplus belongs to the government rather than to its residents, unwinding that precedent is orders of magnitude harder than preventing it.

## Ownership, Not Just Rent: VWHF's Build-to-Sell Track, and What the Dividend Is For

**VWHF's Rail+Property mandate was never meant to be rental-only — building to sell, as well as to hold, is part of how the "Singapore-style wealth fund" framing was meant to be read from the start, and it's worth making that explicit.**

### HDB is mostly an ownership scheme, which is the point of naming it

Singapore's Housing & Development Board, the institution this paper's "Singapore-style wealth fund" framing points to directly, isn't primarily a landlord. Around 80% of Singaporeans live in HDB flats, and roughly 90% of those households own theirs, on a 99-year lease purchased from HDB, rather than rent. HDB builds at cost and sells the overwhelming majority of what it builds to owner-occupiers on long leases, renting out only a residual share to lower-income households. VWHF's mandate, building mixed-income housing at cost on land the state already controls and pricing it below market, was always structured to support exactly that mix: most of what VWHF builds in a precinct can be sold outright to owner-occupiers on long leases the same way HDB's stock is, with the rent-stabilised and market-rate rental dwellings that the rest of this paper focuses on as the share that VWHF retains, the share that funds the turnover clip and the Victorian Dividend. The two tracks were always part of the same mandate; this section just spells out how the sale side works.

### A build-to-sell track, on the same land, under the same mandate

In practice, this means a share of each precinct, the proportion set per-site rather than fixed statewide, is built to the same standard as VWHF's rental stock and sold outright to owner-occupiers on a long-term lease (99 years, mirroring both HDB and the ACT's existing Land Rent Scheme, which already runs this exact structure in Australia). Sale proceeds do two things at once: they return capital to VWHF faster than rental yield alone would, shortening the "seed debt" period the superannuation-barrier section flags as VWHF's real constraint, and they create a cohort of Victorians who own an asset in a VWHF precinct outright, rather than renting one indefinitely.

The conditions that make this work are the same ones the rest of this paper already argues for. Sale is restricted to owner-occupiers, not investors, eligibility could mirror the existing first-home-buyer and key-worker criteria Homes Victoria already uses for its shared equity product, so this track doesn't become another vehicle for the land-banking and speculative holding the Anti-Land-Banking Surcharge elsewhere in this paper is designed to stamp out. Priced at cost plus a modest margin, the same "at cost, undercut the market" logic that governs VWHF's rental stock, a build-to-sell unit in a VWHF precinct is cheaper than an equivalent private apartment for the same reason VWHF's rent is cheaper than market rent: no developer margin, no marketing budget, and land that was already in public hands before the rail line made it valuable.

### Rent-to-equity: a ladder out of VWHF tenancy, for tenants who want it

Not every VWHF tenant wants to move into a build-to-sell unit, and plenty won't be able to even at cost-plus pricing. For tenants who do want a path to ownership without moving, the same shared-equity structure Victoria already runs through the Victorian Homebuyer Fund, the state holds an equity stake (up to 25%, more for eligible single parents and key workers) that the homeowner can buy out over time, can be offered to VWHF tenants directly against the unit they're already living in. A tenant's Victorian Dividend payments, once the tenant-first phase described above is running, can optionally be directed at buying down VWHF's equity share rather than paid out as cash, turning what would otherwise be a recurring rent rebate into a slowly-growing ownership stake in the tenant's own home. Nobody is required to do this, the cash dividend remains the default and the point of the section above, but for a tenant who'd rather build equity than receive a weekly payment, VWHF is both the landlord and the entity best placed to offer that swap, because it already owns the unit and already has the tenant's payment history.

### And the car question: the dividend doesn't come with conditions

The "not needing a car" framing earlier in this paper is about VWHF precincts removing _forced_ car dependency, being walkable and transit-connected enough that going without a car is a realistic choice, not about making car ownership harder or rarer by design. For households who do want a car, whether by preference, for work that depends on one, or because they live somewhere VWHF hasn't built yet, the Victorian Dividend and the rent-discount savings described above are unconditional cash. There's no rule, explicit or implicit, that says this money can't go toward a car loan, an EV purchase, or maintenance on the car a household already has. A household that banks the $112-418/week per-person dividend toward a vehicle is using it exactly as intended, the same way an Alaskan PFD recipient can spend their cheque on whatever they choose.

Where this paper's broader infrastructure agenda intersects with car ownership specifically, it's worth noting it doesn't compete with it. The federal Electric Car Discount (the FBT exemption on eligible EVs bought through salary packaging) already makes EV ownership materially cheaper for any Victorian whose employer offers novated leasing, VWHF's dividend stacks on top of that the same way it stacks on top of a hypothetical federal UBI in the section above, it's additional household cash, not a substitute for, or a precondition against, owning a car.

### The honest framing

The "keeps the rent forever" framing this paper leads with describes the retained share of each precinct, the rental-and-dividend engine the rest of this paper builds on, not VWHF's entire output. Every unit sold rather than retained is a unit that doesn't generate rental income, turnover-clip revenue, or count toward the Victorian Dividend's asset base, which is exactly why the retained share, not the sold share, is what the dividend modelling above is built on. The proportion sold versus retained is a design parameter VWHF's board would set and could adjust precinct-by-precinct, but the split itself isn't a departure from the model, it's what makes VWHF a complete copy of the Singapore model this paper already invokes by name, rather than only the landlord half of it.

The rent-to-equity option carries a smaller version of the same trade-off: every dollar of dividend a tenant redirects into buying down VWHF's equity share is a dollar VWHF's balance sheet effectively pays out early, in the form of foregone future ownership of that unit, rather than holding for compounding rental income. That's the tenant's call to make, not VWHF's, which is why it's offered as an opt-in alongside the cash dividend rather than a replacement for it.

## How This Actually Pays Down Debt: Following the Cash

**This plan doesn't make $199.3 billion disappear. Here's exactly what it does do, and where the cash actually comes from.**

Strip away the structural arguments and ask the blunt question: of the $199.3 billion in net debt this paper keeps citing, how much of it does any of this actually retire, and where does the money physically come from? The honest answer is that this plan runs on two separate tracks, and conflating them is the easiest way for a critic to make the whole thing sound like a shell game — money moving between funds and looking like progress without the headline number changing.

### Track one: cash that goes straight at general government debt

Two of the mechanisms above affect general government debt directly, with no VWHF or ring-fence in between, but they work through different parts of the budget and shouldn't be added together carelessly. The Transport Efficiency Audit ($1 billion a year, phased in on the 2027-2035 timeline set out above as the MR4, Yarra Journey Makers and bus operator contracts reach their natural endpoints) is genuinely new recurring operating revenue/savings, money the Net Operating Balance doesn't currently have, that becomes available to retire debt. The pension amortisation smoothing operates on a different part of the ledger entirely: as set out above, superannuation contributions are a balance-sheet, not operating-statement, transaction under AASB 1049/GFS, so smoothing has no direct effect on the operating balance at all. What it does is reduce, by up to $1.2-1.5 billion a year at its peak, the new debt the state would otherwise have to issue purely to fund the escalating pension top-up under the 2035 schedule, at the cost of roughly $1.95 billion in additional long-run liability from lost compounding. Its contribution to "paying down debt" is therefore really "avoiding additional debt issuance for a purpose that buys nothing", not freeing up money to spend or to retire other debt with. Combined, the two mechanisms should be read as: roughly $1 billion a year in genuinely new operating capacity that can retire debt or fund services, plus a separate, smaller-than-it-looks reduction in how much new pension-related borrowing the state needs to do, achieved at a real long-run cost. Against $199.3 billion in net debt, the genuinely new $1 billion is a little over 0.5% a year on its own — not a figure that makes the headline number collapse, but real, and it compounds: every dollar of debt retired this year is a dollar that isn't accruing interest next year, so the freed-up debt-service cost adds to the pool available for further retirement the year after. Run that for a decade and the figure understates the cumulative effect, because the interest savings are themselves additive to the principal being retired.

### Track two: VWHF's own balance sheet, which is deliberately a different ledger

Everything involving VWHF — the Social Infrastructure Bonds, the 30,000 housing units, the Transit Capital Fund's car park and land-banking surcharge revenue, the Southern Cross commercial take — doesn't flow into track one, and that's by design, not an oversight. This is the same ring-fence discussed in the VWHF section: if this revenue were swept into consolidated revenue to count toward the headline debt figure, it would be competing with hospitals and schools for the same dollar every budget cycle, and the housing mission would be the first thing cut whenever that competition got tight. Keeping it separate is what lets VWHF make multi-decade financing decisions, and it's what eventually funds the Victorian Dividend.

So track two doesn't retire general government debt in cash terms, and pretending otherwise would be the kind of double-counting this document has tried to avoid elsewhere. What it does instead, and this is the honest version of the claim, is stop debt from growing as fast as it otherwise would. The counterfactual to VWHF isn't "the state has tens of billions less debt"; it's "the state borrows at general government rates, competing with every other capital priority, to build 30,000 homes the conventional way — or doesn't build them at all, and the housing crisis (with its crime and cost-of-living flow-on effects) keeps compounding instead." VWHF financing those homes off its own asset-backed balance sheet, even if a state guarantee means the debt technically consolidates back on as discussed earlier, is debt that buys an asset earning rent — the kind this paper argues isn't the problem — rather than debt that's just a cost. The "seed debt is a non-issue" argument from the land-banking section is the asset-side version of this same point: net debt barely moves because it's offset by a land revaluation, which is a balance-sheet statement, not a cash-flow one, and it's a different mechanism from track one's cash-flow retirement.

### Putting the two together, honestly

If someone asks "does this plan cut Victoria's $199.3 billion net debt to some smaller headline number within a few years," the honest answer is no. Nothing in this paper claims that, and any version of this pitch that implied it would be making the same mistake the Reason Foundation accused Mamdani of. What this plan actually does is: (1) generate roughly $1 billion a year in genuinely new operating revenue/savings from the Transport Efficiency Audit, which hits the Net Operating Balance directly and can retire debt or fund services, while separately reducing, by up to $1.2-1.5 billion a year at its peak, the new borrowing the state would otherwise need purely to fund an escalating pension top-up under the 2035 schedule, at a real long-run cost of roughly $1.95 billion in additional liability — neither of these should be read as a combined "$2.1-2.4 billion found" figure, and the pension figure in particular is a reduction in borrowing for a specific purpose, not money available to spend; (2) take the next wave of major capital spending — 30,000 homes and transit-oriented commercial precincts — off the general government borrowing program entirely, financing it instead through an asset-backed fund with its own ring-fenced revenue; and (3) ensure that whatever debt does get taken on, on either track, is the kind secured against, and serviced by, an income-producing asset the state keeps, rather than debt that just sits on the books as a cost with nothing to show for it. That's a meaningfully better trajectory than the status quo, where pension top-up payments and franchise-model leakage both add to the operating shortfall every year with nothing to show for it either. It is not, and shouldn't be sold as, a number that makes $199.3 billion disappear — it's the difference between debt that's a dead weight and debt that's leverage, applied at the margin, every year, compounding.

## Seven More Levers: International Best Practice, and What Victoria Already Has

**Beyond VWHF and the Transit Capital Fund, the state can deploy several entirely fresh, globally tested fiscal and policy levers to clear public debt and self-fund major infrastructure. Some of these build directly on schemes Victoria already runs in a smaller or narrower form; others don't exist anywhere in Australia yet. Both kinds matter, and it's worth being precise about which is which before any of these go into a press release, because "we invented this" and "we're finally catching up to a 1970s French law" land very differently with a hostile fact-check.**

These alternative levers, categorised by their structural mechanisms, are outlined below, each followed by an honest accounting of where Victoria currently sits.

### 1. French-Style _Versement Mobilité_ (Metropolitan Employer Mobility Levy)

Instead of relying on farebox recovery or general consolidated state revenue to service transport debt, the state can legislate a dedicated payroll-based transit levy modelled on the French **Versement Mobilité (VM)**.

- **The Mechanism:** Private companies, public administrations, and state agencies employing 11 or more workers within a designated metropolitan transit boundary are subject to a payroll tax calculated as a percentage of their total wage bill. In France, local transport authorities have the flexibility to adjust this rate within statutory ceilings. For example, in the Paris Île-de-France region, the levy is capped at rates ranging from 1.6% to 2.95% depending on the county.
- **The Financial Impact:** The mobility payroll levy represents the single largest funding source for French metropolitan transit networks, accounting for 51% to 60% of operating and capital budgets.
- **State Implementation:** The state can introduce a localised, tiered transport payroll levy on medium-to-large corporate employers in major metropolitan employment hubs (such as the Melbourne CBD or Clayton industrial zone). Because transit networks expand the labour pool and physical accessibility for these businesses, the levy ensures that the commercial beneficiaries of public transit directly fund its capital and operational expansion.

#### Where Victoria already sits

Victoria has no employer-payroll levy that funds public transport, but it has two of the building blocks already, which is exactly why this proposal should be framed as "finish the job," not "introduce a brand-new tax."

First, Victoria has already proven that a _hypothecated payroll surcharge_ is politically survivable and administratively simple: the **Mental Health and Wellbeing Levy**, in effect since 1 January 2022, adds 0.5% to payroll tax for employers with national payrolls above $10 million (an additional 0.5% above $100 million), with the revenue legally ring-fenced for mental health services. That's the entire VM mechanism, a payroll-based surcharge on larger employers with revenue dedicated to a specific public good, already legislated and collected in Victoria. The only thing missing is a transport-specific equivalent.

Second, Victoria already runs a transport-adjacent levy in the same geography VM would target: the **Congestion Levy**, an annual SRO-administered charge on off-street, non-residential car parking spaces in the Melbourne CBD (Category 1) and inner suburbs (Category 2). From 1 January 2026 these rates rise 73% to $3,030 per space (Category 1) and $2,150 per space (Category 2), with the Category 2 zone expanding into Richmond, Abbotsford and St Kilda Road. But the Congestion Levy is a _parking-space_ charge with no employer-size threshold, and its revenue flows into general consolidated revenue rather than a transit capital fund.

The proposal here isn't "Victoria has nothing like this" — it's that Victoria has the hypothecation precedent (Mental Health Levy) and the geography precedent (Congestion Levy zones) but has never combined them into an employer-payroll levy with transit-specific earmarking. A Victorian Versement Mobilité would sit alongside the Congestion Levy rather than replace it: the Congestion Levy taxes the _supply_ of parking, VM taxes the _payroll_ of the businesses the transit network serves, and directing VM revenue specifically into the Transit Capital Fund closes the hypothecation gap the Congestion Levy currently leaves open.

### 2. State-Owned Public Infrastructure Bank

To bypass the high margins and volatile underwriting fees of international investment banks and commercial lenders, the state can establish a dedicated public bank modelled on the **Bank of North Dakota (BND)**.

- **The Mechanism:** Rather than holding state tax revenues, agency deposits, and municipal cash in private commercial banks, the state mandates that all public funds be deposited into a state-owned public bank. The bank uses these massive consolidated deposits to fund the **Infrastructure Revolving Loan Fund**.
- **The Financial Impact:** The BND provides long-term, fixed-rate loans (set at 2.00% fixed interest for maturities up to 30 years) to local councils and government subdivisions to build wastewater plants, roads, bridges, and emergency facilities. This partnership lending model allows local projects to access highly affordable capital, saving millions of dollars in interest per project. Furthermore, the bank returns a consistent annual profit of approximately $400 million directly to the state treasury.
- **State Implementation:** A state government can establish its own Treasury Public Bank. This bank would leverage the state's consolidated cash reserves to issue ultra-low-interest, long-term financing directly to local councils for community infrastructure, bypassing commercial bank debt and keeping the interest payments compounding within the public sector.

#### Where Victoria already sits

This is the lever where Victoria is furthest along without realising it — and also the one where the gap to BND is most structural, not cosmetic.

**Treasury Corporation of Victoria (TCV)** already does half of this job. TCV is the state's central borrowing and funds-management authority: it raises debt in capital markets at rates that reflect Victoria's strong credit rating, and on-lends to government agencies and, via the Local Government Funding Vehicle and Community Infrastructure Loans Scheme, to councils, at a margin of roughly 0.115% lending fee plus a 0.15% guarantee fee on top of TCV's own cost of funds. That's already a council-facing, cheaper-than-commercial-bank lending channel, run by the state, for infrastructure. It is, in miniature, the lending side of BND.

What TCV doesn't do is the _deposit_ side. BND's defining feature isn't that it lends cheaply, it's that it's legally the custodian of the state's own cash, every agency deposit, every tax dollar in transit, sits in BND rather than a private bank, and that consolidated deposit base is what lets it lend at 2% fixed for 30 years and still return $400 million a year to the treasury. TCV is a debt-issuance and funds-management vehicle, not a deposit-taking bank; Victoria's consolidated cash sits with private commercial banking partners under standard banking arrangements, not with TCV.

So the honest framing is: Victoria doesn't need to invent a public infrastructure bank from scratch, it needs to convert TCV's existing council-lending arm into a true BND-style revolving loan fund by mandating that government agency and council deposits be held with TCV (or a banking subsidiary of it) rather than private banks. That's a much smaller legislative lift than standing up a new institution, because the lending infrastructure, the credit relationships with councils, and the borrowing-cost advantage already exist. The only missing piece is the deposit mandate that would let TCV lend from the state's own cash pool instead of wholesale capital markets, capturing the spread BND captures and returning it to the treasury the way BND does. No Australian state currently operates a deposit-taking public bank, and the federal NAIF (Northern Australia Infrastructure Facility) is a $7 billion project-finance facility, not a deposit-based bank either, so this would still be a first for Australia. But it's a first built on an existing chassis, not a green-field build.

### 3. CBD Cordon Congestion Pricing

To generate a massive, predictable, and non-debt revenue stream to fund capital transit expansions, the state can implement a central geofenced cordon toll, modelled on New York City's **Manhattan Central Business District (CBD) Congestion Tolling**.

- **The Mechanism:** Vehicles entering a designated congestion relief zone during peak hours are assessed a variable toll using electronic E-ZPass or automatic license-plate readers. In New York, the program assesses a $15 base peak toll for passenger vehicles.
- **The Financial Impact:** In its first year of operation, NYC's congestion pricing program generated over $550 million in net tolling revenue. This stream was legally leveraged to unlock $15 billion in capital funding specifically earmarked for transit improvements, including signal modernisations, new passenger railcars, and major subway line expansions. The policy also successfully cut traffic by 11% and regional emissions by 22%.
- **State Implementation:** The state can establish a cordon-tolling zone around the inner-city grid (such as the Melbourne Hoddle Grid or Sydney CBD). Under the enabling legislation, 100% of the net revenues must be directed into an off-budget infrastructure fund to directly pay down the debt of mega-projects like the Suburban Rail Loop or regional high-speed rail.

#### Where Victoria already sits

This one is genuinely missing — no Australian jurisdiction has cordon or vehicle-entry congestion pricing, and it's the cleanest "international best practice we haven't adopted" item on this list.

The closest Victoria has is, again, the Congestion Levy from item 1: a static annual charge on off-street parking _supply_ in the CBD and inner suburbs, last raised 73% for the 2026 year. That's a fundamentally different instrument, it taxes a parking operator once a year regardless of how the space is used, not a driver per trip during peak hours, and it does nothing to directly price the act of driving into the CBD at the times that cause congestion. The debate about whether Victoria should go further has been live for years: the Grattan Institute's _Right time, right place, right price_ report explicitly recommends NSW and Victoria introduce a cordon charge (in the order of $5 on entry and exit during AM/PM peaks) for zones covering the Sydney CBD and the Melbourne CBD, Docklands and Southbank, within five years. UNSW research has separately modelled a $10/day Sydney charge cutting traffic by roughly 40%. Neither state has legislated either.

So this lever has no Victorian or Australian precedent to "strengthen" — it's a straightforward call to adopt a policy that's been recommended by Australia's own infrastructure economists and implemented successfully overseas, but never actually legislated here. The one design choice this paper adds on top of the Grattan/NYC template is the hypothecation: Grattan's proposal is framed primarily as a traffic-reduction measure with revenue as a secondary benefit, whereas NYC's MTA model legally earmarks the revenue for transit capital. This paper follows the NYC model, not just the Grattan one, on that point: 100% of net revenue into an off-budget fund retiring Suburban Rail Loop and regional rail debt, not general revenue.

### 4. Transit Development Districts (TDDs) and Segregated Tax Increment Financing (TIF)

To capture property tax value uplift without imposing new broad-based taxes on existing residents, the state can establish localised **Transit Development Districts (TDDs)** modelled after successful frameworks in Indiana and Illinois.

- **The Mechanism:** A contiguous boundary is drawn around a planned or existing transit station (capped at a maximum area of 320 acres, or half a square mile). Within this boundary, the baseline property tax revenue is frozen.
- **The Financial Impact:** Over the next two to three decades, all growth in property and local income tax revenue above that frozen baseline, the increment, is captured and sequestered into a dedicated fund used exclusively to pay off the public bonds or infrastructure loans issued to construct the rail line and station hub.
- **State Implementation:** Instead of funding new rail extensions out of general state debt, the state treasury can declare every new station corridor a TDD. The land tax and payroll tax "uplift" generated inside the 320-acre zone around the stations is legally isolated to retire the project's construction debt, rendering the infrastructure self-funding over its life cycle.

#### Where Victoria already sits

Victoria has one-off value-capture levies, but not true TIF, and the distinction matters for what this proposal would actually add.

The **Growth Areas Infrastructure Contribution (GAIC)**, in place since July 2010, charges a flat per-hectare fee ($115,530–$137,230 per hectare for FY2025) on rezoning or development of land in Melbourne's designated growth-area municipalities (Cardinia, Casey, Hume, Melton, Mitchell, Whittlesea and Wyndham), raising roughly $225 million a year, with work-in-kind offsets available. Infrastructure Contributions Plans (ICPs), which replaced the older Development Contributions Plans, work the same way: a charge struck once, at the point of rezoning or permit approval. Both of these are exactly the mechanism this document's own Windfall Gains Tax discussion already covers, a one-off transaction trigger, not an ongoing capture of growth.

What Victoria has never legislated is a TIF district in the US sense: a frozen _baseline_ assessed value for a defined area, with the _ongoing, year-on-year growth_ in property tax revenue above that baseline diverted into a dedicated fund for two to three decades. GAIC and ICPs tax the moment of change; TIF taxes the trajectory afterwards. Infrastructure Australia and Infrastructure Victoria have discussed "value capture" conceptually around Metro Tunnel and Suburban Rail Loop precincts, but only as developer levies or one-off land sales, never as a baseline-freeze incremental-capture district.

This is therefore a genuine gap, and it's complementary rather than competing with the existing GAIC/ICP/WGT architecture this paper relies on elsewhere: GAIC and the WGT capture the one-off jump in value at the moment of rezoning, while a TDD/TIF overlay would capture the slower, compounding growth in the years afterwards as the precinct actually develops and matures, the exact growth that GAIC's one-off charge, struck at rezoning, can't see coming. Declaring each new Suburban Rail Loop and Metro Tunnel station corridor a TDD on top of the existing GAIC/WGT triggers would let the state capture both the one-off uplift and the multi-decade growth trajectory from the same precinct, with the TIF increment retiring the station's construction debt specifically rather than flowing into general infrastructure contributions.

### 5. State-Backed Parametric Captive Reinsurance for Clean Transition Infrastructure

State-level captive insurers are historically passive, inward-facing entities used strictly to underwrite state assets and properties against physical damage (such as the Victorian Managed Insurance Authority or the Queensland Government Insurance Fund). However, the state can restructure its captive into an active **Parametric Captive Reinsurance Vehicle** to de-risk green infrastructure investments.

- **The Mechanism:** Public sector entities globally are increasingly deploying captive structures to stabilise risk portfolios. When state utilities or private partners build emerging green technologies, such as grid-scale battery storage prototypes, hydrogen infrastructure, or offshore wind, commercial insurers are often unwilling to provide coverage, or do so at predatory rates.
- **The Financial Impact:** The state's restructured captive can absorb the early-stage, "uninsurable" technology risk, unlocking follow-market commercial capacity and satisfying lender checklists. It can also write structured **parametric covers** to insure against non-damage business interruptions, such as a lack of sun or wind over a multi-year period.
- **State Implementation:** By utilising the state captive to underwrite and pool risk for renewable energy and grid storage projects, the state can significantly lower borrowing volatility and debt costs for green infrastructure developers, accelerating construction timelines without requiring direct cash subsidies.

#### Where Victoria already sits

Victoria has the entity this proposal would restructure, but not the function. The **Victorian Managed Insurance Authority (VMIA)**, established in 1996, is exactly the "historically passive, inward-facing" captive this section describes: it insures government departments, hospitals, schools and cultural institutions against property and liability risk, and advises agencies on managing climate-related risk to _existing state assets_. Nothing in VMIA's current mandate gives it a role writing cover for third-party renewable energy or grid-storage projects, parametric or otherwise, the closest equivalent at state level, Queensland's Government Insurance Fund, appears to operate on the same self-insurance-only basis.

So this is missing in the most literal sense, the legal vehicle exists and is well-established, but its mandate would need to be extended, not invented. It's also worth being upfront that this is the one item on this list with no clean overseas template to point to either: parametric weather-index insurance for renewables is a growing _private_-market product (firms like kWh Analytics and Descartes Underwriting write these covers commercially), but a government captive insurer being restructured specifically to write parametric covers for green infrastructure isn't a model this paper can point to and say "France/Indiana/North Dakota already proved this works." The honest framing is that this would make Victoria a first-mover on the public-sector side of a product category the private market has already validated, lower risk than inventing the underlying instrument, but real first-mover risk on the institutional structure. Extending VMIA's mandate via an amendment to the _Victorian Managed Insurance Authority Act 1996_ is a far smaller legislative step than creating a new statutory authority, which is the main point in its favour.

### 6. Unilateral PPP Termination and Strategic Contract Renegotiation

Before describing the mechanism, a calibration on what this paper does and does not claim about PPPs: there are genuine benefits to the model, and claiming otherwise would be disingenuous. Risk transfer, private sector innovation, and bundled finance-design-build-operate packages have delivered real value in specific contexts. The Southern Cross Station case study elsewhere in this paper is an example of a PPP that, at the time it was structured, represented a reasonable answer to a government that lacked the capital and project-management capacity to build a station of that scale on its own. The critique here is not that PPPs are always wrong, and a blanket "no more PPPs" rule is not what this paper proposes.

The critique is that they have outlived their usefulness in the transport infrastructure contexts Victoria now uses them most heavily for — that the terms have drifted in favour of private partners through successive renegotiations, that the Metro Tunnel experience shows risk transfer is often illusory in practice, and that the accumulated evidence suggests the state is now sophisticated and well-capitalised enough, in part through VWHF, to own these assets directly. There may be individual PPPs genuinely worth keeping, and there may be contexts where the model still makes sense. The PPP Renegotiation Unit described below exists precisely to make that determination systematically, project by project, rather than assuming all existing PPPs should be wound up or defaulting to renewal because change is hard. The goal is a net benefit for the state: let expire what should expire, renegotiate what can be improved, and keep what is genuinely working.

Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs) are highly complex, long-term, incomplete contracts that are frequently renegotiated. Instead of allowing private partners to extract excessive profit margins or shift risks back to the taxpayer during financial distress, the state can implement a highly aggressive, formal **PPP Audit and Renegotiation Framework**.

- **The Mechanism:** Roughly 1 in 3 PPP contracts are renegotiated globally. Historical data (such as Portugal's 254 renegotiations from 1995 to 2012) shows that private firms with political connections frequently use the renegotiation phase strategically to extract rents and drive up public costs. However, well-structured frameworks allow governments to turn the tables.
- **The Financial Impact:** Under strict procurement guidelines, contract managers can determine if fast-paced technological advancements or operational inefficiencies make it economically superior for the government to **unilaterally terminate** a PPP contract, pay out the remaining concession under a pre-agreed formula, and bring the asset fully back under public management. This eliminates ongoing private availability payments and allows the state to upgrade the infrastructure far more cheaply.
- **State Implementation:** The state can establish a permanent PPP Renegotiation Unit within the Department of Treasury and Finance. This unit would systematically audit all active road, school, and transport PPP agreements. When a private partner faces structural financial distress, the state would refuse bailouts, instead utilising its contractual step-in and termination rights to reclaim the asset, restructure the risk, and eliminate the private developer premium entirely.

#### Where Victoria already sits

This is the one item on this list this paper has already, inadvertently, shown working in practice, just not as a deliberate state-driven strategy.

DTF's **Partnerships Victoria** framework already governs every PPP the state signs, and it already includes step-in and default provisions for exactly the scenario this section describes, a private partner in financial distress. Renegotiated PPP deals are also already subject to ex-post review by the Victorian Auditor-General's Office. The Metro Tunnel case study earlier in this paper, the December 2020 "reset" after Cross Yarra Partnership's cost-overrun claims were mediated, where the state ended up contributing up to an extra $1.37 billion towards construction, is a live example of exactly this framework operating, just with the leverage running the wrong way: the renegotiation extracted _more_ money from the state, not less, because it happened reactively, on the private partner's timeline, in response to the private partner's claims.

What Victoria has never done is use the _other_ direction this framework allows: a standing, state-initiated audit programme that goes looking for renegotiation or termination opportunities on the state's terms, rather than waiting for a consortium to come asking for more money. There's no confirmed case of Victoria ever unilaterally terminating a major PPP, and no evidence of a dedicated standing unit within DTF whose job is proactively auditing the existing book of road, school, prison and transport PPPs for step-in or termination value. The legal machinery, step-in rights, default clauses, VAGO review, already exists in every contract; what's missing is an institution whose entire mandate is to use it offensively. Establishing a permanent PPP Renegotiation Unit within DTF is therefore best framed as turning an existing reactive capability into a standing offensive one, the Metro Tunnel reset is the proof that the contractual levers are real, and the proof that, used passively, they currently run in the private partner's favour.

### 7. Whole-of-Government Chief Savings Officer Network

Every lever above targets a specific sector, transport franchises, infrastructure financing, congestion pricing, value capture. The same underlying discipline, a standing, agency-level hunt for savings that doesn't rely on cutting frontline staff or services, extends naturally across the rest of government too, modelled on the **Chief Savings Officer** role New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani established at every city agency in January 2026.

- **The Mechanism:** Each Victorian government department and major statutory agency appoints a Chief Savings Officer, reporting through a central coordinating function inside the Department of Treasury and Finance, with a mandate restricted to procurement consolidation, contract renegotiation, duplicate-system and back-office rationalisation, and process redesign, explicitly excluding headcount reduction, hiring freezes, or frontline service cuts as a savings mechanism. Each agency reports a savings target, and progress against it, publicly every budget cycle, the way NYC's Mayor's Office of Management and Budget now does.
- **The Financial Impact:** NYC's program set savings targets of 1.5% of agency budgets for FY2026 and 2.5% for FY2027, with the administration's own figure, repeated in the May 2026 Executive Budget, claiming a combined $1.77 billion. As flagged earlier in this paper, only around $200 million of that had been independently verified at the time it was reported, with the rest still under review, roughly a 12% confirmation rate against the headline claim. That gap is the central lesson for Victoria, not a reason to dismiss the model: agency-level savings targets are a genuine, low-political-cost lever, but a self-reported target and an audited result can differ by an order of magnitude, and only the audited one should ever be presented as a finding.
- **State Implementation:** The state can legislate the same role into every department and major agency under a Treasury-coordinated framework, with savings reinvested in service delivery or debt retirement rather than returned as a tax cut, and with an explicit, public condition that no target may be met through headcount reduction, position freezes, or cuts to frontline service hours, the same distinction this paper draws elsewhere between structural reform and the Coalition's hiring-freeze approach to "efficiency."

#### Where Victoria already sits

Victoria already has the institutional pieces a Chief Savings Officer network would sit on top of, just not assembled into a standing, accountable structure. The Department of Treasury and Finance runs centralised procurement policy and periodic value-for-money reviews, VAGO conducts ex-post performance audits across departments, and individual agencies run their own internal audit functions. What's missing is the thing Mamdani's model actually adds: a named officer inside every agency with savings as their specific job, a public target each budget cycle, and a mandate structurally barred from reaching for headcount as the easy answer, the exact opposite of the Coalition's "Ten Year Plan," which leans on a back-office hiring freeze claimed, not audited, to save $22 billion, the single largest and least-substantiated line in that plan, as this paper has already shown.

The honest position on the dollar figure is that there isn't one this paper is prepared to claim. Applying NYC's claimed 1.5-2.5% target range to Victoria's $114.5 billion in forecast 2026-27 general government expenses would imply $1.7 billion to $2.9 billion a year, but that number inherits every weakness of the analogy it's built on: it's a foreign city's own contested self-report, of which only around 12% has been independently confirmed, rescaled onto a different government with a different agency structure, different service mix, and zero department-by-department data of its own. That makes it a meaningfully weaker number than even the bus component of the Transport Efficiency Audit above, which at least has a real Victorian payment base to apply a percentage to. Discounted for that, the honest range is probably closer to the low hundreds of millions than the billions, and could genuinely be higher or lower, nobody, including this paper, currently knows, which is exactly the gap a real Chief Savings Officer network would be the first to actually go and find out. This figure is deliberately left out of the Transport Efficiency Audit's total and every other running tally in this paper: it's a structural recommendation with an illustrative ballpark attached, not a number that belongs in anyone's costed total until an actual department-by-department audit produces one.

## The Other Half of the Pitch: What Victoria Actually Needs Built

**This isn't just a balance sheet fix — it's the transport infrastructure Melbourne's growth corridors have been waiting decades for.**

Everything above is about mechanism, smoothing pension schedules, restructuring franchises, capturing land value. It's worth stepping back and saying plainly what all of it is _for_, because the case for VWHF isn't just "the state should own more assets" in the abstract. It's that Victoria has a public transport infrastructure backlog that's actively making people's lives worse, concentrated in exactly the growth corridors where the state has spent the least and the population has grown the fastest, and VWHF's transit-oriented mandate is, among everything else it does, a delivery vehicle for closing that gap.

### The Suburban Rail Loop, finished, not cancelled

The Liberal Democrats' platform, discussed above, gets its headline debt-reduction number by cancelling the SRL outright. This paper takes the opposite position: the full loop, East, North and West, is exactly the kind of nation-building public transport investment Melbourne's growth areas have been waiting decades for, and cancelling it mid-pipeline doesn't just throw away the transport benefit, it throws away the land value uplift around every station on the line that the Transit Capital Fund and VWHF's transit-oriented development model are built to capture. The honest framing isn't "the SRL is free" — it's one of the largest infrastructure projects in Australian history and nothing in this paper pretends otherwise — but every kilometre of it that gets built is also a kilometre of new VWHF development sites with a captured uplift, which is the opposite of how the Liberal Democrats' costing treats it.

### Melbourne's West, North and South East need the same treatment the inner suburbs already got

These are the fastest-growing parts of the state, and the most transport-starved. The problem isn't just "more train lines" in the abstract, it's that growth-area public transport in Melbourne is frequently radial-only: it gets you toward the city, but it does a poor job connecting you to the next suburb over, the shopping strip, the hospital, or the train station that's geographically close but administratively in a different bus route's catchment. Melton is a clear example of this: it is currently easier to get from Caroline Springs to the Woodgrove shopping centre in Melton by public transport than it is to get from within Melton itself to Woodgrove, despite Woodgrove being Melton's own town centre. That's not a one-off planning oversight, it's what happens when public transport networks are built outward from the city rather than around the communities they're meant to serve. VWHF-funded transit-oriented development, paired with a genuine investment in intrasuburban bus networks and the North-South connections growth areas like Melton are missing entirely, is what actually fixes this, not as a side effect of building train stations, but as the explicit point of the mandate.

This isn't just one shopping centre's anecdote. VAGO's June 2026 Bus Plan audit (see "The Bus Plan: A 2031 Promise the Auditor-General Says Won't Be Kept" above) put hard numbers on exactly this pattern across the same growth corridors. Melton sits at 9-12 per cent of its population within reach of high-quality public transport of any kind; Hume and Whittlesea at 21-24 per cent; Cardinia below 1 per cent. Inside Melton, the Rockbank–Mt Cottrell growth area, one of only five communities in outer metropolitan Melbourne with a population over 30,000, has no high-quality public transport within 800 metres at all. These aren't fringe postcodes — they're five of Melbourne's most populous and fastest-growing local government areas, audited by the state's own Auditor-General as falling further behind the Bus Plan's stated ambition, not closer to it.

### The structural reason this keeps happening

This isn't unique to Melton, and it isn't new. VAGO flagged the same dynamic back in 2013, in a dedicated audit of growth-area transport (_Developing Transport Infrastructure and Services for Population Growth Areas_), identifying a roughly $197 million-a-year recurrent funding gap for bus services alone, inside a broader infrastructure and service backlog north of $10 billion, and noting that even the funded arrangements that did exist mostly covered new greenfield estates, not the growth that kept happening in areas a few years after they'd stopped being classified as "new." RMIT's Centre for Urban Research found the same pattern from a different angle in 2024: across Melbourne, apartment numbers grew 88 per cent between 2004 and 2022, while public transport services within walking distance of those apartments grew just 5 per cent; on the Upfield line specifically, nearby apartment numbers grew 134 per cent against a 7 per cent uplift in services. The trigger differs, a new greenfield estate in one case, an apartment tower on an existing line in the other, but the underlying mechanism is the same: the physical capacity to house more people arrives on a planning and construction timetable, while the recurring budget line that pays an operator to actually run more buses or trains through that capacity arrives, if it arrives, on a separate timetable entirely, decided in a different process by a different part of government. Growth doesn't wait for the second timetable to catch up, and historically it hasn't.

Melton South is living through exactly this gap right now. The state's April 2026 "Bus Bonanza," a near-$100 million package folded into the 2026-27 Budget, is real, confirmed money: it funds two entirely new routes for Melton South and a doubling of Route 454's frequency, with a new direct connection from Melton and Cobblebank stations to Woodgrove. That's a genuine, specific answer to the Caroline Springs/Woodgrove gap described above. But funded and running are different things, and transport advocates were quick to label the wider package a "drip-feed" rather than a bonanza, pointing to Route 140, the bus meant to finally connect the isolated Mount Atkinson estate to the rail network: promised in the previous year's budget, still without a confirmed delivery date as of the April 2026 announcement, and not carrying its first passenger (launched as the 140a service) until 28 June 2026, well over a year after it was first funded. As this paper is being written, the Department of Transport and Planning had not published detailed maps or a confirmed start date for the Melton South changes specifically. None of this makes the Bus Bonanza hollow, the money is real and the routes are specified, but Route 140's timeline is the honest benchmark for how long "funded" can take to become "a bus that actually stops outside your house," and Melton South residents are entitled to expect roughly that long before Route 454 reaches Woodgrove's door, not the length of time since the announcement.

### Regional Victoria isn't an afterthought either

A proper regional rail network connecting Geelong, Ballarat, Bendigo and Shepparton, faster, more frequent, genuinely connecting the regional hubs to each other and not just each one individually back to Melbourne, does for regional Victoria what the SRL does for outer-suburban Melbourne: it turns cities that currently function as isolated satellites into a connected network, with all the land-value and economic-activity effects that come with it. VWHF's mandate doesn't have to be Melbourne-only, and a regional rail spine is exactly the kind of asset-backed, land-value-capturing investment the fund is designed to make, particularly given the Shooters, Fishers and Farmers critique above that minor-party transport policy is too Melbourne-centric. There's a version of this pitch that directly answers that critique.

### Health and education aren't VWHF's job, and that's fine, because they don't need to be

None of this is an argument that transport and housing are the only things Victoria is short on; health and education are under exactly the same pressure, and any honest accounting of "what should the state spend more on" has to include both. But VWHF's ring-fenced revenue is, by design, committed to its own mandate, that's the whole point of the ring-fence discussed above. The track-one operating revenue, the roughly $1 billion a year from the Transport Efficiency Audit, is general government money with no such restriction, and is exactly the kind of recurring, no-strings operating cash that hospital and school staffing budgets actually need, as opposed to one-off capital grants that build a building but don't fund the nurses or teachers to run it. The pension smoothing's contribution here is more indirect, by reducing pension-related borrowing it preserves general government debt capacity for these purposes, but it doesn't itself add to the operating envelope. The pitch isn't "VWHF builds everything." It's "stop the avoidable operating bleed where it's real, and the state has room to actually fund health and education staffing increases without that fight being a zero-sum trade against transport and housing."

### Why this all fits together

Every piece of this paper, separately, is a fix for a specific leak: a pension schedule that bleeds cash, a franchise model that hands away fare revenue, land sitting idle near infrastructure the public already paid for. None of those fixes, alone, builds a single train station. But together they're the difference between "Victoria can't afford the SRL, regional rail, and fixing places like Melton" and "Victoria has been bleeding the money that would have paid for those things into pension top-ups, franchise margins, and untaxed land-banking for years." The infrastructure backlog isn't a new spending commitment competing with this paper's other priorities, it's the thing all of this paper's other priorities were always in service of. Stop the bleeding, and the rest becomes affordable on a timeline that doesn't require choosing between a hospital and a train line.

### This is cost-of-living relief, too, and it should be said plainly

Everything above has been framed as debt strategy and asset ownership, but transit-oriented development is one of the largest cost-of-living levers available to the state, larger and more durable than either major party's headline measures. The AAA's Transport Affordability Index puts the average Australian household's annual car costs above $23,000 including loan repayments; even stripping the loan out, fuel, registration, insurance and maintenance alone routinely add up to somewhere in the $8,000-$12,000 a year range per car depending on driving volume, before depreciation. A genuinely walkable, mixed-use neighbourhood, the kind VWHF's transit-oriented precincts are designed to build, doesn't just cut the petrol bill, it can make owning a second car, or a car at all, optional. A full-price annual myki pass for Zone 1+2 costs around $3,700 at the post-2026 daily cap of $11.40. For a household that can drop a car entirely because the train station, the shops and the school are all within walking distance, that's a multi-thousand-dollar annual saving, every year, indefinitely. Compare that to the relief measures both major parties are actually offering: free and half-price transport that expires on an election timetable, or a fuel discount that requires racking up ten toll trips to unlock. Building places where people don't need a car in the first place is cost-of-living relief that doesn't need to be renewed, extended, or campaigned on every cycle, because it's built into the housing stock itself.

It's worth being precise about what kind of relief this is, though, because it's not the same kind the headline measures are. This document is not short-term cost-of-living relief: nobody currently stuck in a car-dependent outer suburb sees a cent of the saving described above next quarter, VWHF precincts take years to plan and build, and none of this puts money in anyone's account this winter. What it does instead is address the structural causes of the cost-of-living crisis, and the housing crisis alongside it, car-dependent growth-area development, untaxed land-banking, the loss of public transport revenue to franchise margins, rather than the symptoms. Short-term relief (rebates, fare discounts, one-off payments) is a legitimate and separate piece of work, and nothing here substitutes for it. But it's worth naming the difference plainly: a rebate is something a government gives you this year and can take away next year; a neighbourhood you can live in without owning a car is something nobody can take away, because it was never a payment in the first place, it's how the place was built.

A companion document, covering short-term cost-of-living relief, the kind of rebates, concessions and one-off payments that reach a household this year rather than over the next decade, is the acknowledged other half of this pitch and is tracked separately (see `SHORT_TERM_RELIEF.md`).

## One Seat, Real Power: What a Fusion MP Actually Does With This

**A single crossbench seat is enough to force every mechanism in this paper onto the public record.**

A single seat in the Legislative Council, or a single lower house seat in a hung parliament, sounds like a rounding error. It isn't, and here's specifically what it looks like in practice:

- **Private member's bill**: A Fusion MP introduces the State Superannuation Act amendment (the 2035-to-2041 amortisation smoothing) as a standalone bill. Even if it doesn't pass on its own, it forces every other party to go on record voting for or against cutting up to $1.2 to $1.5 billion a year of borrowing the state would otherwise need purely to fund an escalating super top-up, with no service cuts attached. That's a hard vote for Labor, who'd rather not admit the 2035 target is unworkable, and for the Coalition, who'd rather not vote for anything that helps the government's books.

- **Budget bill leverage**: In a minority or near-minority parliament, the government needs supply to pass. A Fusion MP with the balance of power makes VWHF's establishment, or the car park land tax surcharge, or the Transit Capital Fund, a condition of supporting the budget bill. This is the standard crossbench mechanism, seen federally with the Greens and teal independents trading votes for specific commitments, and it works because the government's alternative is no budget at all.

- **Upper house committee amendments**: Victoria's Legislative Council committee process lets a single MP move detailed amendments to planning and transport bills. A Fusion MP uses this to insert the 20% inclusionary zoning rule, the cumulative anti-land-banking car park surcharge, or a VWHF public-developer mandate for state-owned transit sites directly into whatever planning bill the government is already running, riding on legislation that's moving anyway rather than waiting for a standalone bill.

- **Question Time and the Public Accounts and Estimates Committee (PAEC)**: A Fusion MP uses PAEC hearings, where ministers and department heads are required to answer on the record, to force the Treasurer to table the actual Core Service Payment figures for Southern Cross, the actual margins Metro Trains and Yarra Trams operate on, or the actual size and basis of the Cross Yarra Partnership's 25-year Metro Tunnel availability payments. This document is built on data that's already public; an MP's job is to make a minister confirm it under oath, in a form the press gallery can quote.

- **The expiry clock as leverage**: Several of these mechanisms, Southern Cross's SDA, the Metro and Yarra Trams franchise renewals, have hard contractual expiry dates already on the calendar. A Fusion MP's job between now and those dates is to keep tabling motions, questions and amendments that put on record, repeatedly, what the government intends to do with these contracts, forcing Labor to either commit to re-municipalisation in writing or be on record defending the PPP model going into an election.

None of this requires forming government. It requires one person with a vote that matters, a clear ask, and the willingness to use procedural leverage relentlessly. That's the entire theory of the seat.

## The Election Narrative

**Four lines that reframe the entire debt debate, without pretending debt doesn't exist.**

This gives Fusion a bulletproof technocratic defence against the standard Liberal "Labor debt disaster" narrative, not by pretending debt doesn't exist, but by reframing what debt is for. Victoria doesn't have a borrowing problem; it has an asset-ownership problem. Every dollar of debt in this paper either retires more expensive debt or buys an asset the state keeps the income from. None of it funds an asset whose returns walk out the door to a private operator.

1. We aren't spending tax dollars: We are using accounting logic to flatten pension debt timelines, just like the progressive leadership in New York.

2. We aren't borrowing from commercial banks: We are building housing through an independent wealth fund using value-generating assets.

3. We are cutting the bloat: We are taking profits back from private transport operators and putting them straight onto the state's bottom line.

4. We are paying it back to you: once VWHF's assets are generating surplus income, a share of it goes out as a direct dividend to Victorians, not just to the budget bottom line.

## Where This Sits Among the Minor Parties — Left and Right

**Where Fusion's plan overlaps with — and sharply diverges from — every other party on the crossbench.**

Fusion isn't the only party arguing the major parties have the debt conversation wrong, and it's worth being precise about where this pitch overlaps with the existing minor-party field and where it doesn't, because the differences are the point.

### The Greens

want a review of rail and tram franchise contracts before renewal, are pushing a mandatory inclusionary zoning bill (30% of new developments of 15+ homes, well above the 20% in this paper, though without the density-bonus offset), and have committed to 100,000 new public homes over a decade with existing public housing kept permanently in public hands. There's real overlap here, especially on inclusionary zoning and on transport operators not simply being handed another contract extension. The difference is mechanism: the Greens' housing target is a build commitment funded the conventional way, through the budget, competing with everything else for capital works dollars. This paper's pitch is that VWHF's ring-fenced, asset-backed financing is what lets a housing build of that scale happen without that competition, and the Greens platform doesn't have an equivalent answer to "where does the money come from" beyond the existing Social Housing Growth Fund and federal co-funding.

### Victorian Socialists

go further on transport, public ownership of trains, trams and buses outright, with "no compensation" to the operators, on the basis that Metro Trains is currently receiving roughly $900 million a year in government payments with, in the Socialists' own words, undisclosed profits. That framing holds up better than a stronger claim would: no VAGO report or parliamentary record discloses MTM's franchise-specific margin, and the one concrete profit figure that has ever surfaced publicly, a 2019 analysis putting MTM's profit at $29 million on $786 million of revenue (a margin of roughly 3.7%), is old, unverified against primary filings, and a long way from "hundreds of millions." The $900 million payment figure itself checks out independently against the value of the MR4 contract (around $6.3 billion over seven years). Their housing platform is the most radical in the field: a public builder constructing 15,000 homes a year, ownership restricted to individuals, not-for-profits and government, a cap of two properties per person, and a five-year rent freeze. The overlap with this paper is the diagnosis, that the current franchise and ownership model is a transfer of public money to private balance sheets, but the prescriptions diverge sharply on method. "No compensation" termination of Metro and Yarra Trams would trigger exactly the contract-breach payouts this paper goes out of its way to avoid by running out the clock instead; and a blanket cap on private property ownership is a different tool to "build a public competitor that prices private land-banking out of existence." Fusion's bet is that out-competing is more durable, and more saleable to a crossbench that has to work with whichever major party forms government, than expropriating.

That said, Fusion sympathises with the no-compensation instinct in one specific and legally meaningful circumstance: where the private partner is already not fulfilling their obligations under the PPP. Termination for convenience — the state ending a contract early because it has decided it no longer wants the private operator — triggers the standard "no worse off" compensation clauses, and those payouts can exceed the value of just running out the clock. But termination for cause — ending a contract because the operator is in material breach of it — is a fundamentally different legal situation. A private partner that is demonstrably failing to meet their contractual obligations has already broken the deal; the state is not morally or necessarily legally obligated to compensate them as if they had held up their end. The Metro Tunnel cost-overrun mediation, the pattern of renegotiations that extracted additional state payments, and any franchise where performance targets are consistently missed rather than occasionally slipped, are all candidate situations where the state should be actively investigating whether a breach-based termination, rather than a convenience-based one, is available. This is an avenue the PPP Renegotiation Unit described in the Seven More Levers section should be mandated to explore for every active PPP — not as a default, but as a genuine option wherever the facts support it.

### Animal Justice Party

has proposed "holding charges" on vacant residential property to push it back into the rental market, which is directionally the same instinct as the cumulative car park surcharge in this paper, tax idle land harder until holding it stops making sense, applied to a different category of property. Their broader housing platform (flexible zoning near transport, smaller and modular housing) is complementary rather than competing with anything here.

### Legalise Cannabis Victoria

doesn't have a developed position on franchise ownership, land tax or a public developer, but its signature revenue proposal, a regulated cannabis market taxed at roughly the same rate as alcohol, is structurally the same move this paper makes elsewhere: find a real, currently-untaxed or under-taxed activity and direct the proceeds at a specific purpose (the Parliamentary Budget Office has costed cannabis legalisation at over $1 billion in revenue plus $250 million in justice-system savings over a decade) rather than rely on either spending cuts or broad-based tax increases.

### On the other side of the crossbench

, the right-leaning minor parties don't just disagree with this paper on detail, several of them disagree with its foundational moves.

### The Liberal Democrats'

most recent costed platform centres on cancelling the Suburban Rail Loop outright and cutting non-frontline public service numbers, which the PBO estimated would improve net debt by roughly $28 billion over the forward estimates, the single largest debt-reduction number of any party surveyed here, achieved purely by not building and not staffing. That's the cleanest possible statement of the philosophy this paper argues against: treat the debt number as the problem to be minimised directly, rather than asking what the borrowing buys. Cancelling a rail project mid-pipeline also forfeits the land-value uplift around its stations that this paper's entire Transit Capital Fund and car-park-surcharge mechanism depends on, there's no value to capture from a line that was never built. The Liberal Democrats' broader low-tax, deregulation-first instincts also put them at odds with the 20% inclusionary zoning rule and the anti-land-banking surcharge, both of which they'd likely categorise as new imposts on property owners and developers.

### Shooters, Fishers and Farmers

(Victorian branch policy currently under review) has historically aligned with other branches' calls to abolish land tax, payroll tax and stamp duty altogether and replace them with a broader-based regime, the diametric opposite of this paper's approach of using land tax-adjacent mechanisms (WGT, VRLT, the car park surcharge) more aggressively, not less. Their regional-Victoria focus, however, isn't incompatible with VWHF in principle: a sovereign wealth fund that builds transit-linked housing and commercial precincts doesn't have to be Melbourne-only, and a future iteration of this pitch that explicitly extends VWHF's mandate to regional rail hubs could find common ground here that doesn't exist on the tax mechanics.

### One Nation Victoria's

housing policy argues government fees, taxes and charges already account for up to 45% of the cost of a new home, and its prescription is to cut those costs, incentivise private developers and NGOs to build affordable housing, and retain negative gearing for small-scale investors. Read against this paper, that's close to a point-for-point inversion: where Fusion wants more land value capture and a public developer that prices at cost specifically to compress the margin available to private developers, One Nation wants fewer charges on developers and a bigger role for them in affordable housing delivery. There's no overlap on the supply-side mechanism, though both diagnoses start from the same observation, that new housing in Victoria costs more than it should, before going in opposite directions on why.

### The DLP

is the partial outlier on the right. Its state platform calls to fast-track residential land release (a supply-side answer that doesn't sit naturally with this paper's land-banking surcharge, which assumes land already zoned for development is the problem, not a shortage of zoned land) and proposes a 25% public transport fare cut funded by cross-subsidy from freight revenue, which at least assumes continued public operation of the network rather than further privatisation. The DLP's stated goal of reducing reliance on foreign debt and halting "the sell-off of Aussie assets" is the one piece of rhetoric on the right of this comparison that rhymes with this paper's core argument, that the state shouldn't be handing the income from public assets to outside operators, even though the DLP frames it around foreign ownership rather than the domestic PPP model this paper targets.

### Family First Victoria

doesn't currently have a developed Victorian platform on any of these issues; its national messaging runs on family-tax-relief and cost-of-living-via-lower-taxes lines that would likely make it cautious about a new land surcharge, but there's nothing specific enough to compare directly.

### What none of the others have

is the dividend, on either side. Every left-leaning minor party's cost-of-living offer is service-based, free or cheaper transport, rent freezes and caps, more public housing stock, and every right-leaning minor party's offer is a tax or charge reduction, lower stamp duty, abolished land tax, fewer development levies. Both are real and defensible, but neither puts money directly into a Victorian's bank account on a recurring basis funded by an asset the state owns. That's not a criticism of either approach; service-based relief and tax relief both reach people in ways a fund that takes years to mature can't, immediately. But it does mean the Victorian Dividend is the one piece of this paper that has no equivalent anywhere else in Victorian politics, major or minor party, left or right, which is exactly the kind of thing that gives a single-seat crossbencher a distinct identity rather than a slightly-different version of an existing one.

It's worth being precise here, because a fair objection is that several parties on this list — the Greens most prominently, but also others at various points — have backed calls for a Universal Basic Income. That's true. The distinction that matters is the one this paper has drawn throughout: a federal UBI is a tax-and-transfer redesign operating at Commonwealth scale, funded by restructuring income tax and welfare payments. The Victorian Dividend is something structurally different — it's a return on state-owned assets, paid by the asset owner (VWHF) to its effective shareholders (Victorian residents), funded by rental income and commercial returns rather than by taxing income and redistributing it. You could have both simultaneously, and they'd stack rather than substitute, as the $1,600/week section above sets out. The point isn't that a UBI is wrong; it's that no other Victorian party is proposing _this_ mechanism — state asset ownership generating a recurring per-person return — which is what makes it genuinely novel ground rather than a repackaged version of something already on offer.

The honest summary: on diagnosis, that public transport contracts and land-banking are transferring public wealth to private actors, this paper is in good company on the left of the minor-party field, and in direct conflict with the right of it, where the dominant view is that taxes and charges on land and development are already too high and should fall further, not rise. On prescription, the Greens' inclusionary zoning and the Socialists' public ownership instinct both point in directions this paper agrees with but funds and sequences differently, betting that an asset-backed fund that competes rather than compels is both more fiscally durable and more achievable from a single crossbench seat; the Liberal Democrats' SRL-cancellation arithmetic shows the scale of debt reduction available to a party willing to simply not build things, which this paper explicitly rejects as the wrong kind of saving. And on the dividend, there's currently no overlap with anyone on either side on _this specific mechanism_, which is either this paper's biggest weakness (untested, easy to dismiss as a gimmick) or its sharpest point of differentiation, depending on how well the "starting narrow, scaling wide" phasing lands. In a hung parliament, that gap — between "cut taxes" and "expand services" and "a federal UBI eventually" — with nobody offering "the state owns assets and pays you a share of the return _now, out of what it already built_" — is exactly the space a single Fusion seat would occupy.

## This Is Step One

**Rail+Property is a forty-year proven model. VWHF is how Victoria learns to run it. Where it goes from here is limited only by whether the incentives stay right.**

The mechanisms in this paper — VWHF, Rail+Property, the Transit Capital Fund, the Victorian Dividend — are not a complete transformation of how the Victorian state relates to its economy. They are step one. They are chosen as step one for a specific reason: Rail+Property has a four-decade track record across multiple cities and multiple economic cycles, which means the institutional knowledge required to execute it already exists and can be imported, the financing structures are understood by ratings agencies and institutional investors, and the political case for it can be made from evidence rather than theory.

Hong Kong's MTR Corporation has run the model continuously since 1980. Tokyo's private railways — Tokyu, Seibu, Keio, Hankyu — pioneered the integrated rail-retail-residential development model even earlier, some of them in the early twentieth century, and those networks are still running on the same basic logic: build the rail line, develop the land it makes valuable, use the property income to keep fares low and the network expanding without ongoing operating subsidies. Singapore's SMRT, Taipei's MRT, Seoul Metro. The model is not experimental. It is, by the standards of public infrastructure finance, conservatively proven.

VWHF is Victoria learning to run this model — building the institutional capacity, the development skills, the commercial leasing expertise, the bond-issuance track record, the precinct management experience, that the state currently outsources to private operators and developers. That capacity, once built, does not have to stop at transit precincts.

The same logic that makes Rail+Property work — a state entity builds infrastructure, captures the value it creates, uses the surplus to fund the next round — applies wherever a public good and a commercial opportunity can be made to point in the same direction. Energy is the clearest next step. A state-owned energy retailer or generator does not need to maximise the return on capital for private shareholders; it needs to cover its costs and deliver reliable, affordable power. The gap between what a state energy entity would charge and what a private retailer charges to cover its margin and shareholder dividend is money that currently leaves Victorian households and does not come back. The _Stadtwerke_ model in Germany — municipal enterprises that bundle energy, water, public transport, and increasingly broadband under one commercial roof, returning surpluses to the municipality rather than to private investors — has operated in cities like Munich, Hamburg and Cologne for over a century on exactly this logic. Healthcare procurement, aged care, essential insurance: anywhere the state is already the buyer of last resort or the regulator of last resort, an entity whose incentives are aligned toward outcomes rather than toward volume has a structural cost advantage over a private operator chasing margin.

The condition that makes any of this work is always the same: the incentive structure has to be right. A state entity whose management is rewarded for cutting costs regardless of service quality, or whose surplus is raided every time the operating budget is under pressure, degenerates into the thing it was meant to replace. The Westfield clip, the Victorian Dividend's statutory ring-fence, the PPP Renegotiation Unit's mandate, and the legal separation between VWHF and the Consolidated Fund are all expressions of the same design principle: the mechanism only works if the financial interest of the entity running it is aligned with the public outcome it is supposed to deliver. Align those incentives correctly, and there is no principled ceiling on what this model can be extended to. Misalign them, and you get every failed state enterprise of the twentieth century. The difference between those two outcomes is not ideology. It is design.

This paper does not cost or design those extensions. That is the work of subsequent papers, and ultimately of a Fusion government rather than a Fusion crossbench seat. But it is worth being explicit that VWHF is designed to be a beginning — the proof of concept, the institution-building exercise, and the demonstration that a state can own productive assets, run them commercially, align the profit motive with the public good, and return the surplus directly to its citizens. Rail+Property is where that demonstration starts, because forty years of evidence across a dozen cities says it works. What it builds toward is limited only by how well the incentives are kept right.

## Transport Rebranding (Research Note — for separate piece)

Victoria's public transport has been rebranded roughly six or more times since 1983. The sequence: **The Met** (1983) → operator-branded chaos after 1999 privatisation (Hillside/Bayside Trains, Connex/M>Train, etc.) → **Metlink** umbrella brand (2003) → **PTV / Public Transport Victoria** (2 April 2012, Baillieu govt) → PTV absorbed into **Department of Transport** (1 July 2019, Andrews govt, but PTV brand retained publicly) → new DTP/Transport logo rollout paused upgrades for 12 months (April 2021) → **Transport Victoria** (website launched 15 August 2024; PTV dissolved 31 July 2025; PT content migrated 6 August 2025). Daniel Bowen titled a 2014 post _"Rebranding number 6 (in 20 years): Can we stick with 'PTV' for a while please?"_

**Metlink → PTV (2012):** ~80,000 signs across 28,000 bus stops, 4,000 tram stops, 350 stations (logo panels swapped, not whole signs). Ventura Bus Lines alone: 494 buses repainted orange over ~2 years from August 2014. VLocity trains from June 2014. Trams gradual green livery. myki cards rebranded November 2013; machines received new stickers only in July 2016. Design/wayfinding by Diadem (Sydney). **No total cost was ever publicly disclosed.**

**PTV → Transport Victoria (2024–25):** Same physical scope — ~80,000 signs again, full vehicle livery cycle, app, myki. Official rationale: PTV website was end-of-life and needed reinvestment regardless; consolidating with roads under one portal. **Again, no total cost publicly disclosed.**

**The gap:** No Victorian Auditor-General report has specifically audited transport rebranding expenditure across any of these changes. No FOI appears to have forced the figures out.
